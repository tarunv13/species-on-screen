What We Observe

We trace the invisible threads between the animals that appear on our screens and the ecosystems they call home. Three lenses guide this observation.

🎥

Species on Screen

How species appear in human media and culture - films, documentaries, news coverage, social media, literature, and advertising. Every representation is a data point in the cultural record of how we see the living world.

🌱

Ecological Reality

What is actually happening to these species in the wild. Population trends, habitat loss, threat assessments, and conservation status paint a picture that often diverges sharply from the stories we tell on screen.

🔗

The Connection

How cultural representation may shape conservation outcomes. Does screen attention translate to conservation action? Does media portrayal match ecological truth? These are the questions the observatory exists to explore.

Panthera tigris

Tiger

Fewer than 4,500 remain in the wild

Habitat

Tropical and subtropical forests across Asia. Their range spans 13 countries: India, Russia, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Myanmar, Laos, China, Cambodia, and Vietnam.

Key habitats include the Sundarbans mangroves of Bangladesh and India, Siberian boreal forests in Russia, Sumatran rainforests in Indonesia, and tropical dry forests of central India.

Their historical range covered most of Asia. Today, tigers occupy less than 7% of their original territory - fragmented islands of wilderness in an ocean of human development.

Cultural Presence

Cinema: Life of Pi (2012), The Jungle Book (1967, 2016), Two Brothers (2004), Tiger Zinda Hai (Bollywood), Roar (1981)

Literature: Shere Khan in Kipling's The Jungle Book, Hobbes in Calvin and Hobbes, The Tiger by John Vaillant, Life of Pi by Yann Martel

Mythology & symbolism: Chinese zodiac (Year of the Tiger), Hindu deity Durga's mount, Korean folk symbol, William Blake's "The Tyger"

Media presence: Tigers appear in advertising more than any other wild cat - cereal boxes, sports teams, national symbols of India, Malaysia, South Korea, and Bangladesh.

Cultural paradox: The tiger is simultaneously one of the most culturally visible and ecologically threatened large mammals on Earth.

Threats

Habitat fragmentation: Agricultural expansion, logging, and infrastructure development continue to carve tiger habitat into ever-smaller pieces.

Poaching: Driven by illegal wildlife trade, demand in traditional medicine, and trafficking of bone and skin products across international borders.

Human-wildlife conflict: As habitat shrinks, tigers and humans increasingly compete for space, leading to retaliatory killings and community fear.

Climate change: Sea level rise threatens the Sundarbans (home to approximately 100 tigers), while changing monsoon patterns affect prey availability.

Prey depletion: Overhunting of deer and wild boar by local communities removes the tiger's food sources, forcing them into closer contact with people.

Conservation Status

IUCN Red List: Endangered (since 1986, downlisted from Critically Endangered for some subspecies following recovery efforts)

Population trend: Increasing globally - estimated approximately 3,200 in 2010, approximately 3,900 in 2016, and approximately 4,500 in 2022.

Major recovery programs: India's Project Tiger (since 1973), the TX2 global goal to double wild tigers by 2022, and numerous national action plans.

Three subspecies extinct in the 20th century: Bali tiger (1950s), Javan tiger (1970s), Caspian tiger (1970s).

Six subspecies remain: Bengal, Indochinese, Malayan, Siberian, South China (functionally extinct in the wild), and Sumatran.

When Richard Parker gazed from the screen in Life of Pi, 63 million viewers saw a tiger. In that same year, fewer than 3,500 remained in the wild. The question this observatory asks: does seeing translate to saving?

Every Story Shapes Our Response

The Eco-Cinema Observatory will connect two worlds of data: the cultural record of how species appear on our screens, and the ecological record of how they fare in the wild. By mapping these connections across thousands of species and millions of media appearances, we can begin to understand how human attention relates to species fate.
