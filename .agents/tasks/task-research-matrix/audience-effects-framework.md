# Audience Transformation Framework: Digital Biodiversity Media

> A research-grade analysis of how different forms of digital
> biodiversity content shape audiences across distinct cognitive,
> affective, and behavioural outcomes.
>
> **Scope.** Twelve major media forms, each analysed against an
> eight-field schema. Companion document to
> `species-coverage-typology.md` in the same research-matrix
> directory.
>
> **Status.** Working draft. Intended to support: literature reviews,
> conservation-communication research, comparative media studies,
> doctoral methodology design, digital culturomics work, serious-
> games-for-conservation studies.
>
> **Not.** This is not project doctrine, not steering, not
> implementation specification. It does not bind PRs. It is a
> research-analysis reference.

---

## Preamble

### The four-construct distinction

The single most consequential methodological commitment of this
framework is to keep the following four constructs **operationally
distinct**. The conservation-communication literature has accumulated
substantial evidence that these constructs do not reliably co-occur,
and that media forms which excel at one frequently underperform at
others. Conflating them — particularly conflating awareness or
emotional engagement with behavioural change — is the dominant
source of inflated impact claims in this field.

| Construct | Definition | Typical instruments | Common confusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Awareness** | Cognitive recognition that an entity, issue, or species exists; ability to recall or recognise on prompt | Recall tasks, recognition tasks, search-data spikes, name-listing | Confused with *understanding* |
| **Emotional engagement** | Affective response to exposure: valence, arousal, expressive markers, self-reported feeling | Self-report scales (PANAS, etc.), physiological measures, facial coding, qualitative interview | Confused with *attitude change* and with *action* |
| **Ecological understanding** | Comprehension of causation, mechanism, structural relationships, transferable models of how ecosystems work | Causal reasoning tasks, transfer tasks, prediction tasks, structured free-recall | Confused with *awareness* or with verbal fluency on a topic |
| **Behavioural change** | Measurable, durable change in action that persists beyond the exposure context and is ecologically relevant | Longitudinal behaviour tracking (donation, purchase, voting, volunteering), behaviourally relevant paradigms | Confused with *intention*, with self-report of behaviour, or with one-off action ("warm glow") |

For each media form below, these four constructs are tracked
separately. A media form that produces high awareness and high
emotional engagement but low ecological understanding and negligible
behavioural change is *not* a successful conservation-communication
form by any operational definition; it is a form that has captured
attention without converting it.

### Methodological orientation

The framework draws on, and is intended to be legible to, the
following adjacent literatures:

- **Knowledge-attitude-behaviour (KAB) gap research** —
  Kollmuss & Agyeman (2002) "Mind the Gap" remains foundational;
  the value-action gap and intention-behaviour gap literatures in
  environmental psychology (Steg & Vlek; Schultz et al.).
- **Persuasive-communication and behaviour-change frameworks** —
  Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen); the Transtheoretical Model;
  Stern's environmentally significant behaviour framework.
- **Critical nature-documentary studies** — Bousé "Wildlife Films"
  (2000); Mills "Showing and Telling"; Chris "Watching Wildlife".
- **Conservation marketing and flagship research** — Verissimo et
  al.; Smith & Sutton on flagship species effectiveness; Wright et
  al. on conservation social media.
- **Climate emotion / doom fatigue literature** — solastalgia
  (Albrecht); eco-anxiety scholarship; compassion-fatigue research
  (Moeller; Figley).
- **Hope vs fear appeals** — Hornsey, Bain et al. on the limits of
  fear-based environmental messaging.
- **Parasocial-interaction research** — Horton & Wohl (1956);
  contemporary applications to YouTube and short-form creators.
- **Algorithmic-attention studies** — Tufekci, Zuboff, Bucher.
- **Persuasive games and procedural rhetoric** — Bogost; Gee;
  Sandbrook et al. on conservation games.
- **VR and empathy claims** — Bailenson; Markowitz et al.; meta-
  analytic critiques of "empathy machine" claims.
- **Citizen science participation and learning** — Bonney et al.;
  Dickinson et al.
- **Environmental journalism** — Boykoff; Painter on climate
  reporting.
- **Conservation culturomics** — Ladle, Correia, Jaric and
  collaborators.

### Operational rules

1. **Distinguish constructs at every level of analysis.** When the
   evidence supports awareness gains but not behavioural change, the
   document says so explicitly.
2. **Treat self-reported behaviour with caution.** Stated intention
   and self-reported behaviour systematically overestimate actual
   behaviour change (Vesely & Klöckner; Lange & Dewitte).
3. **Treat short-term studies with caution.** Most documented
   effects in conservation-communication research are measured days
   to weeks after exposure; durable effects beyond six months are
   substantially under-measured across all media forms.
4. **Acknowledge the spectacle-transformation gap.** A media form
   can be highly successful at attention capture and highly
   successful at producing affect, while failing to produce
   structural understanding or durable behaviour. These are
   independent dimensions.
5. **Do not generalise from successful cases.** "Blue Planet II
   plastic effect" claims, "Cecil effect" claims, and similar
   high-profile cases are partial, contested, and frequently used
   to support broader claims that the underlying evidence does not
   warrant.

### Field schema

Each of the twelve media forms is analysed against the following
fields:

1. **Dominant emotional strategy** — the affective register the
   form characteristically deploys.
2. **Attention structure** — the temporal and cognitive shape of
   exposure (length, density, voluntary/algorithmic, focused/ambient).
3. **Memory effects** — what is retained, for how long, in what
   form (episodic, semantic, procedural, parasocial).
4. **Conservation strengths** — what the form is empirically or
   theoretically well-positioned to produce.
5. **Conservation distortions** — characteristic misrepresentations
   the form imposes on its biodiversity subjects.
6. **Behavioural effects** — what evidence exists, and what its
   limits are.
7. **Risks of spectacle over understanding** — how the form may
   substitute attention capture or aesthetic engagement for
   ecological literacy and durable change.
8. **Research opportunities** — productive open questions and
   under-studied areas.

The schema is stable. Future amendments preserve it.

## 1. Nature documentaries (long-form, broadcast tradition)

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Awe, wonder, narrative arc.
Affect is structured by the documentary form itself: rising
tension, behavioural climax, narratorial reflection. Grief is
permitted but contained; outrage is rare; hope is reserved for
closing frames. The Attenborough register — measured authority,
restrained sympathy — has substantially shaped audience
expectations of what a nature documentary "feels like".

**2. Attention structure.** Long-form (typically 50 minutes per
episode), linear, choreographed. Audience attention is sustained
by professional pacing; the form is well-suited to viewers
already inclined toward sustained ecological viewing. Background
viewing is increasingly common in streaming contexts but the form
is designed for full attention.

**3. Memory effects.** Episodic memory of vivid sequences is high
and durable: viewers can recall specific shots — the snow leopard
hunt, the iguana-vs-snakes sequence — years later. Semantic
memory of facts is more modest. Place memory is moderate;
specific species names beyond charismatic taxa are weakly
retained.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Sustained attention budget allows
ecosystem context to be conveyed alongside species behaviour.
Production budget enables footage no other form can produce.
Scientific authority of narration provides epistemic credibility.
Recurring exposure across decades has built shared cultural
reference points.

**5. Conservation distortions.** Anthropomorphism in narration and
edit (Mills, Bousé). Dramatisation of behaviour through editing
(occasionally outright staging — the BBC "Frozen Planet" polar bear
in zoo controversy is the canonical case). Charismatic-megafauna
selection bias. Ecosystem politics — extractive industries,
conservation conflict, Indigenous land rights — systematically
under-represented in favour of "pristine nature" framings.
"Blue chip" stylistic homogenisation across global production.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Search behaviour spikes briefly after
broadcast and returns to baseline within days to weeks. Donation
spikes are observable around specific high-profile broadcasts.
The "Blue Planet II plastic effect" — frequently cited as a
behaviour-change exemplar — is partially supported in awareness
data but the durable behavioural component is contested in the
literature. Documented effects are typically short-term and shallow
relative to claimed cultural impact.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Substantial.
Documentary form trains audiences to expect ecology delivered as
narrative entertainment. Audiences emerge with strong episodic
memories and confident self-assessment of ecological knowledge that
is not matched by structural comprehension. Awe-saturated viewing
can produce *aesthetic* engagement without *ecological*
understanding (Mills's "showing and telling" critique).

**8. Research opportunities.** Longitudinal effects beyond six
months are systematically under-studied. Cross-cultural reception
of UK/US-produced documentaries in Global South audiences is
under-studied. The relationship between documentary viewing and
*structural* ecological understanding (as distinct from species
familiarity) is a productive open question. Comparative analysis
of same-species coverage across multiple landmark productions is
methodologically tractable.

## 2. Streaming platforms (Netflix, Disney+, Apple TV+, etc.)

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Inherits broadcast
documentary's emotional grammar but recontextualises it within an
algorithmic catalogue alongside scripted entertainment. Affect is
modulated by recommender placement (a documentary recommended
after a thriller produces different priming than the same
documentary recommended after a children's programme).

**2. Attention structure.** Variable. Binge-mode is possible but
ecological attention in binge mode is low. Background viewing is
common. Discovery is algorithmic and thumbnail-driven; the
audience for any given documentary is shaped by what the platform
chose to surface, not by editorial conservation logic.

**3. Memory effects.** Diffuse. Viewers frequently cannot recall
which platform a documentary was on, which series an episode
belonged to, or which broadcaster produced it. The "Netflix
documentary I watched once" pattern produces awareness and
emotional engagement of the moment but weak indexed retention.
Memory of individual species and places is comparable to
broadcast viewing; memory of *attribution* is substantially worse.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Reach. Streaming platforms have
underwritten substantial new conservation documentary production
(Our Planet; My Octopus Teacher; A Plastic Ocean). Global
audience scale is unprecedented. Subscription model removes some
of the demographic limits of broadcast.

**5. Conservation distortions.** Documentaries framed as
"content" alongside drama, true-crime, and entertainment;
boundary between editorial and entertainment registers blurs.
Recommender-bias toward "what performs" creates feedback loops
that reinforce existing charismatic preferences. Thumbnail and
title selection optimised for click-through, not for ecological
framing.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Less durable than broadcast. The
fragmented viewing context (interleaved with non-documentary
content; viewed across multiple sessions) appears to weaken
attitude consolidation, though direct comparative studies are
limited. Donation and search-behaviour effects observed but
typically smaller than for major broadcast events.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Substantial.
Streaming context aligns documentary viewing with entertainment
consumption logic; conservation content competes for attention
with optimised entertainment, and the emotional grammar of the
platform surrounds it. The documentary itself may be excellent;
the consumption context erodes its ecological gravity.

**8. Research opportunities.** Recommender-system effects on
biodiversity-content exposure are almost entirely unstudied. The
"Netflix Effect" research literature has focused on fiction;
comparable work on documentary is sparse. Cross-genre placement
priming (does watching a thriller before a nature documentary
shift its reception?) is a tractable experimental design.

## 3. YouTube ecology content

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Variable by channel. Educator
register (Real Science; Veritasium-adjacent ecology content) emphasises
clarity and competence. Field-channel register (Brave Wilderness,
TierZoo, Coyote Peterson) emphasises encounter, discovery, and
parasocial warmth. Activist register (Mossy Earth, Climate Town)
combines explanation with direct calls to action. The platform does
not impose a single emotional grammar.

**2. Attention structure.** Variable: 5-minute explainers to
multi-hour deep-dives. Subscription model creates *return*
visitation, which is rare in broadcast and streaming. Audiences
return because of the creator, not the topic; this is a
significant structural difference from documentary forms.

**3. Memory effects.** Parasocial-anchored memory is durable. A
viewer will remember "Tom Scott's video about [X]" or "what
TierZoo said about [Y]" in ways they would not remember a
broadcast equivalent. The creator becomes a mnemonic anchor; the
species or topic is indexed through them. This produces *deeper*
species memory in some niches but *narrower* breadth.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Genuine deep niches: entomology,
mycology, herpetology, marine biology, taxonomy. Long-form is
permitted by the platform in a way it is not on most others.
Creators with genuine expertise can sustain audiences over years.
Non-charismatic taxa receive substantial coverage in specialist
channels. Comments and community features support secondary
learning.

**5. Conservation distortions.** Monetisation pressures shape
content: dramatic titles, exotic-pet content, "what would happen
if" formats, sponsorship inflation. Algorithmic incentivisation
of certain framings (encounter-and-danger formats outperform
quiet observation). Some channels with apparent conservation
framings have welfare-problematic underlying practices (handling,
baiting, captive-display).

**6. Behavioural effects.** Stronger than passive media because
of parasocial trust. Creator recommendations measurably shift
purchase behaviour, donation behaviour, and (in conservation
contexts) volunteer-recruitment outcomes. The same parasocial
trust can also propagate misinformation; the channel-quality
distribution is high-variance.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Moderate. The form
permits structural explanation in a way short-form does not, but
many channels select for spectacle in their thumbnail and opening
seconds (algorithmic survival pressure). Within high-quality
channels, the spectacle risk is comparatively low; across the
platform as a whole, it is significant.

**8. Research opportunities.** Comparative effectiveness of
creator-led vs institutional conservation content is under-studied.
The political economy of monetisation and its effect on conservation
content is methodologically tractable through comparative content
analysis. Long-tail subject coverage on YouTube vs broadcast is a
clean culturomics question.

## 4. TikTok wildlife virality

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Peak-emotion micro-doses:
delight, surprise, disgust, fascination with strangeness. The
short format compresses affect into seconds; sustained emotional
arc is unavailable. Anthropomorphic captioning and audio overdubs
amplify emotional reading.

**2. Attention structure.** Seconds-long, algorithm-driven feed.
Voluntary deep engagement is minimal; the platform is structured
for endless scroll. The relationship between viewer and
individual video is shallow; the relationship between viewer and
platform is deep.

**3. Memory effects.** Paradoxical. Memory of any individual
video is low, but memory of *species* exposed through TikTok can
be high — a viral capybara or sea-bunny becomes more memorable
to a young audience than a documentary segment, partly because
of repetition across many short videos. Memory is species-level,
not behaviour-level or ecology-level.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Reach into demographics other
forms do not reach. Non-charismatic species sometimes go viral
(mantis shrimp; sea bunny; tardigrade) and are introduced to
audiences who would not otherwise encounter them. Speed of
information propagation can be useful for time-sensitive
conservation alerts.

**5. Conservation distortions.** Out-of-context behaviour clips
(slow-loris defensive posture as "cute waving" is paradigmatic);
pet-trade pressure on viral species is well-documented (axolotl,
pangolin, slow loris, capybara, fennec fox); misinformation
propagation (incorrect species ID, fabricated facts overlaid on
real footage); welfare-problematic content (animals filmed in
inappropriate conditions).

**6. Behavioural effects.** Documented short-term effects on
search behaviour, e-commerce activity (toys, merchandise, exotic
pets), and museum/zoo visitation. Documented behavioural harm
through pet-trade pressure on viral species. Durable
conservation-supportive behaviour change is sparsely evidenced;
viral attention rarely converts to durable engagement.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Very high. The form
structurally privileges spectacle; ecological context is
practically unavailable in seconds. Even creators committed to
educational content must compress to peak-affect format to
survive the algorithm. Viewers acquire species visibility
without ecological literacy.

**8. Research opportunities.** Viral-cycle longitudinal analysis
correlated with pet-trade interest data (Google Trends, online
marketplace data, customs seizure records) is a clean
cross-source design. Cross-cultural variation in viral wildlife
canons is almost entirely unstudied. The relationship between
short-form attention spikes and durable attitude change is a
critical and under-evidenced question for this form.

## 5. Instagram wildlife aesthetics

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Aesthetic appreciation;
beauty; aspiration. Wildlife framed as desirable image rather
than as living subject. Emotional register skews toward affirmation
and admiration; concern, decline, and ecological complexity are
under-represented.

**2. Attention structure.** Brief but repeated; feed-mode. Stories
add ephemeral context. Reels (Instagram's TikTok analogue)
imports short-form video grammar. The platform's identity as a
*lifestyle* platform shapes biodiversity content as
lifestyle-adjacent rather than as conservation media.

**3. Memory effects.** Image-led; species names often disconnected
from images. The "I've seen this animal on Instagram" pattern
produces visual recognition without species knowledge. Place
memory is geographic-aspirational ("I want to go there") rather
than ecological.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Photographer-led visibility for
rare or hard-to-photograph species. Visual fidelity is high; image
quality far exceeds short-form video. Photographers with genuine
field experience can build educational audiences. Some
conservation organisations have used the platform effectively for
campaign visibility.

**5. Conservation distortions.** "Instagram-able" wildlife trips
drive measurable tourism pressure on specific sites and species
(geo-tagged location pressure on raptor nests, predator dens,
nesting beaches). Baited shots and staged photography are an
ongoing ethics issue (the "wildlife photographer" influencer
genre includes welfare-problematic practices). The
photogenic-in-still-image bias is severe and excludes most
small-bodied, cryptic, and behaviourally subtle species.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Documented effects on travel
decisions, photography-equipment purchases, and photographer
emulation. Documented harms through tourism pressure on specific
sites and through emulation of unethical practices.
Conservation-supportive behaviour change is occasionally
documented around well-designed campaigns; durable broad effects
are not well-evidenced.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Severe. The form is
optimised for image rather than for context; ecological
understanding is structurally difficult. The aspirational frame
positions wildlife as *destination* and *acquisition* rather than
as *neighbour* or *kin*; this is a non-trivial obstacle to
conservation literacy.

**8. Research opportunities.** The relationship between
geo-tagged Instagram exposure and tourism pressure on specific
ecological sites is a tractable, important, and under-studied
empirical question. Comparative analysis of conservation NGO
Instagram strategies and their measurable outcomes is also open.

## 6. Conservation campaigns

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Urgency; scarcity; hope-
tinged-with-loss; empowerment-through-donation. Campaigns
typically front-load emotional appeal and back-load action
prompts. "We can still save them" is the canonical structure;
fear-appeals without efficacy components are increasingly
recognised as counterproductive (Hornsey, Bain et al.) and have
been somewhat reduced in recent professional practice.

**2. Attention structure.** Short, repeated, campaign-cyclical.
Coordinated across multiple platforms simultaneously (paid social,
email, donor mailings, sometimes broadcast spots, hashtag-driven
events on key dates: World Tiger Day, World Pangolin Day, Earth
Day).

**3. Memory effects.** Brand-and-stat retention is high among
engaged audiences ("tigers doubled in [country]" via the TX2
campaign is a paradigmatic example). Campaign-specific phrases
("the last [N] [species]") become anchored. Memory beyond the
sponsoring organisation's framing is more variable.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Coordinated messaging at scale.
Measurable funds (campaigns are designed to be measurable).
Demonstrable policy outcomes in some cases (anti-ivory campaigns
contributing to legislative change; pangolin-trafficking awareness
contributing to CITES decisions). Capacity to mobilise rapid
response.

**5. Conservation distortions.** Campaign success is typically
measured in fundraising rather than in ecosystem outcomes;
outcome-success and fundraising-success are loosely correlated at
best. "Single-species solutionism" — the campaign frames the
ecosystem as the species. Donor fatigue and campaign-burnout
cycles are well-documented. Multiple campaigns competing for the
same donor attention flatten into generic urgency, weakening each.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Donation behaviour is well-documented
and short-term effects are strong (campaign timing produces
donation spikes). Sustained behaviour change is weaker; "warm
glow" donation patterns (Andreoni; Lee et al. on charity
behaviour) suggest that single donations frequently substitute
for, rather than catalyse, broader behaviour change. Voting and
advocacy effects are documented for sustained campaigns with
clear policy targets.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Moderate to high.
Campaign communication is by professional necessity simplified
and emotionally optimised; ecological complexity is reduced.
"Hope" framings designed to convert can substitute for
structural understanding. Audiences can develop strong campaign
loyalty without ecological literacy.

**8. Research opportunities.** Campaign-effectiveness vs
visibility decoupling — i.e., is this campaign producing
ecological outcomes commensurate with its visibility? — is
substantially under-studied because evaluation is conducted by
the campaigning organisations themselves. Independent
longitudinal evaluation across multiple comparable campaigns
would be high-value research.

## 7. Environmental journalism

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Concern; alarm; occasionally
hope or solutions framing. Journalistic register constrains overt
emotional appeal; the form's authority depends on apparent
impartiality. Emotional engagement is achieved through
specificity (a named place, a named person, a named loss) rather
than through aesthetic.

**2. Attention structure.** Text-dominant; image and video
adjuncts. Reading attention is more demanding than viewing
attention; readership is smaller and more self-selecting. Episodic
event coverage dominates (a fire, a flood, a die-off, a policy
announcement); ongoing coverage of structural conditions is
under-resourced in most outlets.

**3. Memory effects.** Better than visual media for specific
facts, named policies, named actors, and causal mechanisms. Worse
for visual species memory. The reader who follows environmental
journalism systematically tends to develop more
*structurally* organised ecological knowledge than the viewer who
consumes equivalent volumes of visual nature media.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Causal explanation; structural
critique; ongoing accountability coverage of policy and
extractive industries; capacity to platform Indigenous, local,
and structural-justice perspectives that documentary forms often
omit. Investigative work produces durable evidentiary records.

**5. Conservation distortions.** "False balance" on questions
where the scientific evidence is asymmetric (climate-change
journalism's historical struggle with this is well-documented:
Boykoff). Episodic coverage privileges dramatic events over
chronic conditions. Publication bias toward tractable narratives
(individual villains, clean victories) over ecological
complexity. Newsroom resourcing for environmental coverage has
declined across most markets.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Voting, advocacy, donation, and
career-choice effects among engaged readers are documented.
Audience size is the limiting factor: behavioural effects per
reader can be substantial, but reach is narrow compared to
mass-market visual media. Readers of environmental journalism
disproportionately self-select into pro-conservation
demographics, creating selection-effect challenges for
impact research.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Comparatively low,
relative to other forms in this framework. The journalistic
register prioritises explanation over spectacle. Risks emerge at
the social-media-distribution layer (where headline and image
travel without the article) and at the publication-business layer
(where dramatic events outperform structural coverage).

**8. Research opportunities.** The transition from print
readership to social-media-mediated journalism has substantially
changed audience exposure patterns; the conservation-relevant
effects of this shift are under-mapped. Audience studies of
environmental-journalism readers as conservation actors are
limited. Comparative coverage analysis across regions and
languages is methodologically tractable.

## 8. Interactive documentaries

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Variable by author. Common
modes: contemplative exploration; investigative immersion;
witness-position documentary. The form is small enough as a
genre that emotional grammar is author-driven rather than
form-driven.

**2. Attention structure.** Non-linear, depth-on-demand,
explorer-mode. Audience agency in pacing and pathway. Sessions
tend to be longer than single-video viewing but shorter than
multi-episode documentary commitment. Friction of navigation is
substantial relative to passive forms.

**3. Memory effects.** Agency-mediated. Material the viewer
*chose* to engage with shows higher retention than equivalent
imposed material in linear forms. Path-personalisation produces
distinctive viewer-specific memories — different viewers
remember different aspects of the same i-doc, which is both an
asset (durable salient memory) and a measurement complication.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Multi-path narrative permits
depth without forced linearity; well-suited to subjects with
complex causal structure. Capacity for serious primary-source
integration (interviews, data, archival material). Research
material can be embedded in the artefact.

**5. Conservation distortions.** Production cost is high; small
audience reach. Friction reduces casual engagement; users who
don't already self-select for slow exploration disengage. The
form has not stabilised commercially; many i-docs become
unmaintained over time.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Limited evidence base. Studies that
exist (MIT Open Documentary Lab; emerging i-doc research) suggest
agency increases engagement quality but the relationship to
durable behaviour change is not well-established.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Comparatively low.
The form structurally privileges contextual depth over peak
moments. Risk emerges when interactive features are added for
novelty rather than to serve content; "interactive" in such cases
becomes a spectacle of its own.

**8. Research opportunities.** Comparative effectiveness of
linear vs interactive treatment of identical conservation content
is a cleanly designable experiment. Long-term retention
following i-doc engagement is almost entirely unstudied.
Authoring-tool effects on i-doc design (do the available tools
shape what stories get told?) is a productive
political-economy-of-tools research direction.

## 9. Serious games (conservation- and ecology-focused)

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Agency; mastery; occasionally
moral weight (when game systems implicate the player in ecological
harm). Procedural rhetoric (Bogost) — meaning conveyed through
game-system structure rather than through narrative — is the
form's distinctive emotional-cognitive mechanism.

**2. Attention structure.** Extended sessions; replay possible;
commitment is voluntary and frequently substantial (hours to
tens of hours). Cognitive load is higher than passive forms;
this self-selects for engaged audiences.

**3. Memory effects.** Mechanism-bound retention is high: players
remember *how systems work* in a way passive viewing rarely
achieves. Specific facts retention is variable. The "I learned
ecology from [game]" effect is real but bounded by the game's
modelled mechanisms.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Experiential learning of system
dynamics: trophic cascades, population dynamics, ecosystem
feedback loops. Capacity to simulate counterfactuals (what
happens if we remove this species, change this policy, alter
this parameter). Designed pedagogy permits explicit alignment
with conservation-literacy goals.

**5. Conservation distortions.** Game-design constraints
prioritise tractable, simulatable mechanisms over messy reality;
the game's model of ecology is necessarily reductive. "Gamification
of nature" critique: framing ecology as an optimisation problem
imports problem-solving logic that may not transfer well to real
ecosystems. Reward-loop fitting (the game must remain rewarding to
play) can compromise pedagogy.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Domain-specific evidence: serious
games on specific conservation topics show measurable knowledge
gains and short-term attitude shifts (Sandbrook et al. and
adjacent literature). Transfer to non-game behavioural change is
weaker but documented. Effects in target audiences (those not
already interested in conservation) are under-evidenced relative
to effects in receptive audiences.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Variable and
form-specific. A well-designed serious game produces
*understanding* by its core mechanism; a poorly designed one
produces game-mastery without ecological transfer. The risk is
not spectacle in the visual sense but *system-mastery as
substitute for ecological understanding* — the player learns the
game's model without recognising it as a model.

**8. Research opportunities.** Durability of attitude change
beyond six months is under-studied. Effects in
non-self-selecting audiences (students assigned a game, vs
volunteer players) are systematically under-measured. The
transfer of game-learned ecological models to real-world
reasoning is a well-defined experimental question that has not
been adequately addressed.

## 10. Non-commercial / art games (indie, contemplative, ecological)

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Contemplation; melancholy;
ambient attention; resistance to dominant gaming affect (mastery,
competition, completion). Examples: Walden, a game (Tracy
Fullerton); Mountain (David O'Reilly); Eco; Endling — Extinction
is Forever; A Short Hike; Beyond Blue.

**2. Attention structure.** Often slow; sometimes deliberately
non-interactive (Mountain is famously near-non-interactive).
Sessions are variable; replay value is design-dependent. The form
self-selects strongly for niche audiences who tolerate
non-standard game grammars.

**3. Memory effects.** High for design-rare experiences; the
particularity of art-game design produces durable memory. The
player remembers having played Endling years later in a way they
may not remember a generic open-world game.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Design space for *ecological
subjectivity* — playing as a non-human entity, occupying
non-human time-scales, experiencing non-anthropocentric
perspectives. Resistance to extractive game logics opens space
for non-commodifying ecological framings.

**5. Conservation distortions.** Small audiences; preaching to
the converted; production-budget limits. The art-game audience
is heavily Anglophone and skews demographically narrow. Critical
reception within the form sometimes prioritises art-discourse
over conservation-effectiveness.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Research-sparse. The audience size is
small enough to make population-level studies difficult. Case
studies and qualitative research exist; quantitative behaviour
studies are rare.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Comparatively low,
inverted from mainstream forms: art games are more likely to
under-produce engagement than over-produce spectacle. The risk is
of inaccessibility rather than of attention capture without
substance.

**8. Research opportunities.** Almost everything in this
category is under-researched. The relationship between
non-anthropocentric game design and ecological-perception change
is a substantial open question. Comparative studies across art
games would benefit from concerted methodological development;
the genre is well-bounded and tractable as a corpus.

---

## 11. VR / immersive systems

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Presence; embodiment; awe;
empathy claims. The "VR as empathy machine" rhetoric (Milk; the
TED-circuit position) substantially exceeds the empirical
evidence for durable empathy effects (Bailenson; Markowitz et
al.; meta-analytic critiques).

**2. Attention structure.** Intense but bounded. Sessions are
typically short (15-30 minutes) due to motion sickness, equipment
weight, and headset social-acceptability. Intensity within the
session is high; duration across the session is constrained.

**3. Memory effects.** High for episodic experience: viewers
recall VR experiences with the specificity of lived events ("I
was in the Antarctic / underwater / inside a coral reef").
Memory is structured by embodied perspective rather than by
narrative; this is distinctive among the forms in this
framework.

**4. Conservation strengths.** Scale conveyance: the size of a
blue whale, the depth of an ocean canyon, the scale of a
deforested landscape can be experienced in ways no other form
permits. Perspective-shift: whale-eye-view, insect-scale, soil-
column views are technically possible. Awe-induction is
empirically robust; awe has documented downstream effects on
prosocial behaviour (though VR-specific durability is less
clear).

**5. Conservation distortions.** "Empathy machine" claims
overstated. Novelty effects confound short-term studies (effects
attributed to VR may be effects of unfamiliar technology).
Equipment-access bias: VR audiences are demographically narrow.
Production cost limits species coverage; VR content for
biodiversity is heavily skewed toward charismatic, photogenic,
and large-scale subjects.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Short-term attitude shifts and
prosocial-behaviour effects are documented in laboratory
studies. Long-term effects are sparsely evidenced and methodology
is inconsistent across the literature. Specific conservation-
behaviour studies (donation, advocacy, sustained engagement)
remain limited.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** High, particularly
in current production practice. The novelty and intensity of VR
make it well-suited to spectacle and comparatively poorly suited
to nuance. The form's strengths (scale, perspective) can be used
to convey ecological structure but frequently are not; producers
default to charismatic awe.

**8. Research opportunities.** The "VR and empathy" literature
needs longitudinal replication with conservation-behaviour
outcomes as dependent variables. Comparative studies of
ecological vs anthropocentric VR (does it matter whether the
VR puts you inside an ecosystem or above it?) are essentially
unstudied. Equity-of-access questions (who can experience
conservation VR?) are politically and methodologically important
and under-engaged.

## 12. Citizen science platforms

**1. Dominant emotional strategy.** Pride; ownership; community
membership; flow-state in observation tasks. The emotional
register is participatory rather than spectatorial, which is a
fundamental structural difference from every other form in this
framework.

**2. Attention structure.** Task-bound; can be sustained over
years. iNaturalist users frequently make thousands of
observations; eBird users similarly. Attention is field-mediated
(observations occur outdoors, not on screen) with platform
attention serving as recording and learning support.

**3. Memory effects.** Very high for actively-observed species —
the "iNaturalist effect": species that the user has personally
identified become substantially more memorable than species
encountered through passive media. Identification skill
development is a documented outcome (Dickinson et al.).

**4. Conservation strengths.** The form produces actual
ecological data used in research, conservation policy, and
biodiversity monitoring. Participants gain genuine taxonomic and
ecological expertise. Observational practice itself trains
attention — a behavioural change that is intrinsic to the
activity rather than downstream of it. Community knowledge-
sharing supports learning at scale.

**5. Conservation distortions.** Data-quality concerns
(misidentifications, geographic-bias of observers, taxonomic-
expertise asymmetries). Dominant-language bias in platform
interface and community. Volunteer demographics skew (age,
income, region, language). Identification-easy species are
over-recorded; cryptic species are under-recorded.

**6. Behavioural effects.** Among the forms in this framework,
citizen science has the strongest documented record of
producing durable, ecologically-relevant behaviour change.
Identification skill, attentional habit, conservation-supportive
behaviour, and continued engagement are well-documented over
multi-year scales. The effect is partly self-selection
(participants are already inclined) but the *learning* and
*deepening* effects are robustly evidenced.

**7. Risks of spectacle over understanding.** Comparatively low.
The form structurally privileges observational practice over
spectacle. Risks emerge at the gamification edge — leaderboards,
species-counting competitions, badges — which can import
collection logic into otherwise observational practice.

**8. Research opportunities.** Comparative effectiveness across
citizen-science platforms (iNaturalist, eBird, Zooniverse,
Globe at Night, etc.) is partially studied but not systematically
synthesised. Equity of participation is a substantial open
question. The transfer of citizen-science learning to broader
ecological literacy and to non-observational conservation
behaviour is a productive line of work.

## Cross-cutting analysis

### Comparative summary against the four constructs

The following table represents an indicative synthesis of the
preceding analyses. It is intended as a navigational aid for
research design, not as a finalised empirical claim. Each cell
should be read as "the form *characteristically* produces this
level of this construct, given current evidence and current
production practice." Better-than-typical individual works exist
in every form.

| Form | Awareness | Emotional engagement | Ecological understanding | Behavioural change | Spectacle risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nature documentaries | High | High | Low–Moderate | Low–Moderate (short-term spikes) | High |
| Streaming platforms | High | Moderate | Low | Low | High |
| YouTube ecology | Moderate–High (channel-dependent) | Moderate | Moderate–High (channel-dependent) | Moderate (parasocial-driven) | Moderate |
| TikTok virality | High (volume) | High (peak) | Very low | Low — and frequently *negative* (pet trade) | Very high |
| Instagram aesthetics | High (visual) | High (aesthetic) | Low | Low — and frequently *distorting* (tourism) | Very high |
| Conservation campaigns | High | High | Low–Moderate | Moderate (donation; sometimes durable) | Moderate–High |
| Environmental journalism | Moderate | Moderate | High | Moderate (advocacy, voting; small reach) | Low |
| Interactive documentaries | Moderate | Moderate–High | Moderate–High | Limited evidence | Low–Moderate |
| Serious games | Moderate | Moderate | High (mechanism-bound) | Moderate (domain-specific) | Low–Moderate |
| Non-commercial / art games | Low (small audience) | Variable, can be deep | Variable | Sparse evidence | Low |
| VR / immersive | Moderate (limited reach) | High (presence) | Moderate (production-dependent) | Modest, contested | Moderate–High (novelty-confounded) |
| Citizen science | Lower volume but Deep | Moderate (pride) | Very high | High (well-documented) | Very low |

### The emotion-behaviour gap

Every form in the upper portion of the table — nature
documentaries, streaming, TikTok, Instagram, campaigns, VR —
produces emotional engagement that substantially exceeds its
ecological understanding output and its behavioural change
output. This pattern is the conservation-communication
equivalent of the well-established environmental KAB gap and
should be expected, not lamented, in the design of communication
strategies.

The forms that close this gap most reliably — citizen science,
serious games, environmental journalism — share three properties:

1. **Active cognitive engagement** is required to participate.
2. **Structural or causal content** is the medium, not an
   add-on.
3. **Sustained or repeated engagement** is structurally
   supported.

Forms that lack these three properties should not be evaluated
against behavioural-change criteria. A nature documentary that
produces awe and species recognition is doing what its form
does well; failing to produce durable behaviour change is not a
defect of the documentary, it is a property of the form.

### Spectacle vs transformation

Spectacle is attention captured by sensory or emotional intensity
without structural cognitive engagement. Transformation is
durable change in how the audience perceives, understands, or
acts in relation to the subject.

The two are not opposites and frequently coexist. They are also
not interchangeable, and one does not reliably produce the
other. The conservation-communication field has accumulated
substantial evidence that high-spectacle forms often produce
low-transformation outcomes; this should be treated as a baseline
expectation in evaluation design.

A research-mature platform analysing media effects should:

- Specify *which* of the four constructs each measurement targets.
- Treat awareness and emotional engagement as *necessary but
  insufficient* conditions for transformation, not as proxies
  for it.
- Reserve "behaviour change" claims for studies that meet
  longitudinal, ecologically-relevant, and behaviourally-validated
  criteria.

### Aesthetic engagement vs ecological understanding

A particular failure mode in conservation media is the production
of *aesthetic ecological subjectivity*: an audience that has been
trained, by repeated exposure, to recognise nature primarily as a
source of beauty. This is a stronger claim than "spectacle over
substance." It is the suggestion that some forms — Instagram
aesthetics most clearly, but high-production documentary and VR
can drift here too — train an aesthetic relationship with
biodiversity that may actively *interfere* with ecological
understanding by positioning the audience as appreciator rather
than as participant in or component of ecosystems.

This is not a settled empirical claim; it is a hypothesis with
methodological tractability. Comparative studies of audiences
heavily exposed to aesthetic-mode biodiversity content vs
participatory-mode biodiversity content (e.g., citizen science
participants) on ecological-understanding instruments would be
valuable.

### Attention capture vs long-term attitude change

Attention capture is well-measured, behavioural-relevant data
collection (clicks, watch time, search spikes, donation timing).
Long-term attitude change requires longitudinal designs, careful
control for self-selection, and behaviourally-validated outcome
measures.

The asymmetry between the *availability* of attention-capture
data and the *availability* of long-term-attitude-change data
distorts the conservation-communication evidence base.
Practitioners and researchers should treat this asymmetry as a
methodological constraint to be designed around, not as a
substantive finding. The fact that a media form produces a
measurable attention spike does not constitute evidence for its
durable conservation impact; the absence of long-term data does
not constitute evidence against it.

## Closing notes

### Use

This framework is designed to support:

- Literature review structuring across conservation-communication,
  nature-documentary, environmental-communication, and
  digital-culturomics traditions.
- PhD methodology design — particularly studies that seek to
  isolate one or more of the four constructs.
- Comparative analysis of conservation campaigns and conservation-
  oriented media across forms.
- Pre-registration of expected effects: studies designed against
  this framework can specify *which* constructs they expect to
  measure and *which* they explicitly do not address.
- Critical evaluation of conservation-communication impact claims
  in policy, fundraising, and academic contexts.

### Limits

This framework is descriptive, not predictive. It synthesises
existing literatures and identifies open questions; it does not
resolve them. The empirical base for many of the claims here is
unevenly developed; some forms (citizen science, broadcast
documentary) have mature literatures, while others (TikTok,
non-commercial games, VR for biodiversity) have thin or
emerging evidence bases. Treat the table cells accordingly.

### Companion artefacts

- `species-coverage-typology.md` — the species-level taxonomy
  that this audience-effects framework complements. Cross-tagging
  a media artefact through both — what species categories does
  it represent, what audience effects does its form produce —
  yields a two-axis classification useful for corpus
  construction.
- A future habitat-coverage typology and a narrative-technique
  taxonomy are flagged as natural further companions; both are
  out of scope for this document.
- A bias-measurement framework — operationalising the
  representational and effects gaps quantitatively — is the
  highest-priority companion artefact for any platform that
  intends to measure its own coverage and impact.

### Maintenance discipline

Future amendments to this document should:

- Preserve the eight-field schema for each form.
- Date and justify any added or merged form, and any movement of
  forms within the comparative table.
- Distinguish empirical updates (new evidence) from interpretive
  updates (new framing of existing evidence).
- Cite at least one source for any claim that goes beyond
  consensus in the cited literatures.
- Resist expansion of form-count without clear analytical need.
  The current twelve forms cover the major ecosystems of
  digital biodiversity content; sub-form proliferation reduces
  the framework's analytical traction.
