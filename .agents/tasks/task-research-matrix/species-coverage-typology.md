# Species Coverage Typology

> A research-grade taxonomy of how wild species are represented across
> digital media ecosystems.
>
> **Scope.** This document classifies *representational categories*, not
> taxonomic groups. A single species typically occupies multiple
> categories simultaneously; this is a feature, not a flaw. The
> taxonomy is multi-tag.
>
> **Status.** Working draft. Intended to support: dataset construction,
> comparative media-corpus studies, conservation-communication gap
> analysis, digital culturomics, and PhD-level research design.
>
> **Not.** This is not project doctrine, not steering, not implementation
> spec. It is a research apparatus reference. It does not bind PRs.

---

## Preamble

### Methodological orientation

The taxonomy organises species according to *how digital culture
encounters them*, not how taxonomy or ecology classifies them. The
categories below are drawn from, and intended to be legible to, the
following adjacent literatures:

- Conservation marketing and flagship-species theory (Smith & Sutton;
  Verissimo et al.).
- Conservation culturomics — the use of digital data sources to
  measure cultural salience of species (Ladle, Correia, Jaric and
  collaborators).
- Conservation communication and environmental media studies
  (Hansen; Cox; the journal *Environmental Communication*).
- Critical nature-documentary studies (Bousé, Mills, Chris).
- Internet-popularity-vs-conservation-status studies (Donaldson et al.;
  the "wow effect" literature).
- Aesthetic-bias studies in conservation funding (Smith & colleagues
  on the "ugly" deficit; Adamo et al.).

### Operational rules for using this taxonomy

1. **Multi-tag by default.** A tiger is simultaneously a flagship
   megafauna, a charismatic predator, a culturally mythologised species,
   a conservation-campaign species, a conflict species, and a
   documentary-icon species. Single-tagging is almost always an error.
2. **Tags travel with media artefacts, not just species.** The same
   species can appear in different categorical configurations across
   media items (a panda in a WWF campaign vs. a panda in an Animal
   Crossing context vs. a panda in a Sichuan ethnographic film).
3. **Categories are descriptive, not normative.** "Internet-viral
   species" is not a value judgement; it is a media-circulation status.
4. **The taxonomy is revisable.** Categories may be added, merged, or
   retired as digital media ecosystems evolve. Each amendment should be
   dated and justified in this file.
5. **Visibility is asymmetric across the taxonomy.** The categories are
   not equal in coverage volume; this asymmetry is itself a research
   variable.

### Field structure

Each category is described against ten fields:

1. Definition
2. Why it becomes culturally visible
3. How digital media shapes perception
4. Conservation risks of overrepresentation
5. Conservation risks of underrepresentation
6. Example species
7. Typical ecosystems associated with the category
8. Dominant emotional framing
9. Dominant narrative framing
10. Research implications

The fields are stable. Future amendments should preserve them.

## 1. Flagship megafauna

**1. Definition.** Large-bodied vertebrate species deliberately
selected by conservation organisations as ambassadors for broader
biodiversity, ecosystem, or campaign goals. The category was formalised
in conservation marketing literature in the 1990s but the practice
predates it (e.g., the WWF panda logo, 1961).

**2. Cultural visibility.** Visibility is a function of human visual
preferences (large body size; forward-facing eyes; perceived
intelligence; neotenous features in some species), pre-existing cultural
investment, and decades of compounded media exposure. Megafauna are not
chosen because they are charismatic in the abstract — they are
charismatic *because* they have been chosen and amplified.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Saturated representation across all
media tiers: documentary, news, social, fundraising, branding, game
mascots. The same species recur across unrelated platforms, producing
a feedback loop in which megafauna familiarity reinforces megafauna
selection. Algorithmic systems amplify what is already amplified.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Skews global conservation funding
disproportionately toward a small set of taxa (well-documented since
Clucas et al. 2008). Encourages "celebrity ecology" in which an
ecosystem is pitched through a single hero species. Renders
co-occurring biodiversity invisible. Creates "shifting symbolic
baselines" — when a flagship's status declines, the surrounding
ecosystem's collapse may be misread as a single-species story.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Almost never underrepresented.
Mentioned for completeness only.

**6. Example species.** Giant panda, tiger (all subspecies), African
elephant, polar bear, Bornean orangutan, blue whale, mountain gorilla,
snow leopard, black rhinoceros.

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Tropical rainforest, savanna, alpine,
Arctic, temperate forest, open ocean. Notably absent from this list:
freshwater systems other than the largest, soil ecosystems, deep
ocean, microbial systems, cave systems.

**8. Emotional framing.** Awe; paternalistic concern; loss; pride;
identification with national or institutional symbols.

**9. Narrative framing.** "The last [N] remaining"; the lone hero
species defending an ecosystem; the species as moral protagonist;
the symbolic stand-in for a region.

**10. Research implications.** Flagship megafauna function as a
control category for media-bias studies — the question is not whether
they are over-covered (they are) but *how much*, *with what stylistic
inheritance*, and *how their coverage volume compares to ecologically
equivalent or more important taxa*. Useful for benchmarking digital
culturomics signal.

## 2. Charismatic predators

**1. Definition.** Large-bodied, often apex carnivores whose visibility
draws on perceived danger, dramatic behaviour, and cultural fascination
with predation. Substantial overlap with flagship megafauna but
distinguished by the centrality of *predation behaviour* to their media
identity.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Predator-prey dynamics are intrinsically
filmic — they have a beginning (stalking), a middle (chase), and an
end (capture or escape). Predators activate human attention systems
(threat detection) more reliably than herbivores. Long mythological
investment: hunter, sovereign, devil, ancestor.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Predator coverage is structurally
biased toward dramatic events: hunts, kills, near-misses. Patient,
non-dramatic behaviour (resting, social grooming, prey-rejection)
under-circulates. Algorithmic systems amplify the dramatic clip,
producing an over-representation of "predator at peak" and an
under-representation of "predator at rest."

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Reduces ecosystems to predators-
plus-backdrop; suppresses prey species, plant communities, and
non-vertebrate ecology. Reinforces the "trophy" narrative that
historically licensed extermination. Encourages mediated human-
predator conflict cycles in which dramatic incidents become the
public-relations frame for whole species.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Limited; charismatic predators
are rarely underrepresented relative to their ecological footprint.

**6. Example species.** Tiger, lion, leopard, snow leopard, jaguar,
gray wolf, great white shark, killer whale, brown bear, harpy eagle,
saltwater crocodile.

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Savanna, temperate and boreal forest,
mountain, ocean (pelagic and reef-edge), wetland, alpine.

**8. Emotional framing.** Fear-suffused-with-admiration; danger;
sovereignty; respect; ambivalent attraction.

**9. Narrative framing.** The hunt; the king; the killer; the
encounter. Recurrent character archetypes: the lone male; the mother
defending cubs; the scarred elder.

**10. Research implications.** Predator-suspense is one of the most
quantifiable narrative structures in nature media. Useful for studies
on attention budgets, on the propagation of single dramatic clips
across platforms, and on how predator framing transfers (or fails to
transfer) to non-predator species presented under predator-style
cinematography.

## 3. Keystone species

**1. Definition.** Species whose presence or absence has a
disproportionate effect on community structure relative to their
abundance (Paine 1969). The concept is ecological, but the term has
substantially leaked into popular media in the past two decades.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Variable. Some keystone species are
charismatic enough to be visible (sea otter, gray wolf in
Yellowstone); many are not (parrotfish, Pisaster ochraceus, prairie
dogs in some regions). Visibility lags ecological importance.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** The "trophic cascade" explanation has
become a popular-science genre, particularly after the Sustainable
Human "How Wolves Change Rivers" video (2014), which established a
template subsequently applied (and over-applied) to many systems.
Visibility tends to attach to a small set of keystone case studies
that are repeatedly cited as exemplars.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** "Trophic cascade" becomes a
single explanatory frame; ecosystems with diffuse, multi-species
keystones are flattened into simple causal stories. Wolves-in-
Yellowstone is paradigmatic and partially contested in the
ecological literature, but the popular version remains dominant.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Many ecosystems are sustained
by non-charismatic keystones (decomposer fungi, specific
invertebrates, microbial communities) that media systems struggle
to render. The ecological insight does not transfer to the species
that need it most.

**6. Example species.** Sea otter, gray wolf, beaver, parrotfish,
Pisaster ochraceus (ochre sea star), African elephant, fig trees,
flying fox, prairie dog, bison.

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Kelp forest, riparian, intertidal,
tropical reef, savanna, temperate grassland, dipterocarp forest.

**8. Emotional framing.** Revelation; surprise at disproportion;
respect; "small species, big effect" affect.

**9. Narrative framing.** The cascade; the ripple effect; the
keystone-pulled-and-arch-falls metaphor; the missing piece.

**10. Research implications.** Trace whether the keystone frame, when
applied in popular media, transfers to genuinely non-charismatic
keystones or whether it stays attached to a stable canonical set.
A useful longitudinal indicator of whether ecological literacy is
deepening or remaining ornamental.

## 4. Ecological engineers

**1. Definition.** Species that physically modify, maintain, or
create habitats through their activities, thereby controlling
resource availability for other species (Jones, Lawton & Shachak
1994). Frequently overlaps with keystone species but is functionally
distinct: keystones change *food-web structure*, engineers change
*physical structure*.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Visible engineers are charismatic
(beavers, corals); invisible ones are most engineers (earthworms,
termites, soil microbes, peat-forming sphagnum, certain bivalves).
The category's media presence is thin relative to its ecological
weight.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Time-lapse of habitat modification is
filmic and well-suited to short-form platforms; "watch a beaver
build a dam" is reliable content. Slow, large-scale engineering
(coral reef formation, peat bog accumulation, soil aggregation) is
under-represented because its time-scale exceeds short-form attention
budgets.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** The visible engineers (beavers,
corals) become the "type" of engineer in public imagination,
crowding out the small-bodied and microbial engineers that do most
of the planet's engineering by mass.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Loss of soil engineers,
invertebrate reef-builders, and microbial mat-formers receives
minimal cultural registration; their decline can proceed without
public awareness.

**6. Example species.** Beaver, scleractinian corals (staghorn,
brain, elkhorn), Pacific oyster, eastern oyster, earthworm
(Lumbricus), termite (multiple genera), prairie dog, sphagnum moss,
mangroves, dung beetles.

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Wetland, riparian, coral reef, intertidal,
soil, leaf litter, peatland, grassland.

**8. Emotional framing.** Industriousness; quiet transformation;
slow time; competence.

**9. Narrative framing.** Architects, builders, world-makers,
unsung labourers.

**10. Research implications.** The engineer category is one of the
most useful for *gap analysis*: comparing the volume of beaver and
coral coverage to earthworm and termite coverage produces a
quantitative measure of digital media's bias toward visible
engineering work.

## 5. Invasive species

**1. Definition.** Non-native species whose introduction causes, or
is likely to cause, ecological, economic, or human-health harm. The
ecological meaning is technical (introduced plus damaging); the
media usage is frequently looser and conflates non-native with
invasive.

**2. Cultural visibility.** "Villain" framing is highly filmic and
generates clear narrative structure. Invasive-species media is a
defined sub-genre of conservation journalism and documentary.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** "The invasion" framing inherits from
warfare and (more troublingly) from human-migration discourse.
Coverage clusters on visually striking invasions (Burmese pythons in
the Everglades, lionfish in the Atlantic, brown tree snakes on
Guam). Slow, plant-led, microbial, and pathogen-driven invasions
under-circulate despite often higher ecological cost.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Adoption of xenophobic
metaphor structures into ecological discourse; selection bias toward
"warlike" invaders; under-engagement with the messy ethics of
restoration (when "removal" means killing established animals).
Also: the invasive label can be applied to species whose status is
ecologically debated, freezing public perception in advance of
science.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Non-charismatic invasions
(pathogenic fungi devastating amphibians; chestnut blight; emerald
ash borer; zebra mussels; soil microbial introductions) receive
disproportionately low coverage relative to ecological cost.

**6. Example species.** Burmese python, lionfish, brown tree snake,
cane toad, kudzu, Asian carp, zebra mussel, emerald ash borer,
Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (chytrid fungus), feral cat (in
island ecosystems), house sparrow (in some contexts).

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Islands; freshwater systems; novel
ecosystems; agricultural and peri-urban interfaces.

**8. Emotional framing.** Alarm; militancy; resentment; occasional
ambivalence (animal welfare in eradication).

**9. Narrative framing.** Invasion; war; eradication; the line
that must be held.

**10. Research implications.** Corpus-linguistic analysis of
invasion metaphor (war terms, xenophobic patterns) is well-
established in environmental communication and is a productive area
for computational text studies. Comparison of media volume against
ecological cost is a clean dataset.

## 6. Climate-symbol species

**1. Definition.** Species adopted, formally or informally, as
visual symbols of climate-change impacts on biodiversity.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Climate change is abstract; symbol
species concretise it. The polar bear on shrinking ice is the
canonical case, established in the late 1990s and early 2000s and
intensively reproduced thereafter.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Stock-image saturation: the same
small set of images recirculates across decades of climate
journalism. Misattribution is common — a starving polar bear may be
attributed to climate change without the causal chain being
established. Climate-symbol species often become *the* image of
climate change in places where the species does not occur.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Ecological causation flattens
into a single climate frame, eclipsing other co-occurring threats
(habitat loss, pollution, hunting, disease). "Symbol fatigue" — the
audience stops registering an image after extended repetition —
reduces communication effectiveness over time. The symbolic load
can outpace the species' actual ecological situation.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Tropical, freshwater, and
microbial impacts of climate change have weaker symbol-species and
correspondingly lower public salience. Coral bleaching is the
significant exception in tropical systems.

**6. Example species.** Polar bear, emperor penguin, scleractinian
corals (in bleaching contexts), American pika, Adélie penguin,
monarch butterfly (in some framings), red knot, walrus.

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Arctic, Antarctic, alpine, coral reef,
montane, migratory corridor.

**8. Emotional framing.** Grief; abstract loss; helplessness;
moral implication.

**9. Narrative framing.** Canary in the coal mine; the last refuge;
the melting world; the disappearing species.

**10. Research implications.** Symbol stability over time is
measurable through reverse-image search frequency and stock-image
licensing. Tracking "symbol fatigue" cycles and the emergence of
successor symbols is an open longitudinal research question.

## 7. Extinct / extinction-symbol species

**1. Definition.** Species used to symbolise biodiversity loss,
either historically extinct (with cultural memory persisting) or
recently/imminently extinct (with media memorialisation in
progress).

**2. Cultural visibility.** Extinction has the cleanest narrative
arc available — a beginning, a middle, and a definitive end. The
arc is morally unambiguous, which makes extinct species resilient
public-communication subjects.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Historical reconstruction
(illustration, AI-generated "footage", museum-collection imagery,
de-extinction speculation). Memorial-style coverage at the moment
of declaration. Recurring "anniversary" coverage of well-known
extinctions.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Public attention concentrates
on a small canon of charismatic extinct species (dodo, thylacine,
passenger pigeon) while ongoing routine biodiversity loss
proceeds invisibly. Nostalgia becomes the dominant mode, displacing
prevention. The "extinction is past" assumption.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Invertebrate, plant, fungal,
and microbial extinctions almost never reach popular consciousness.
Extinction events involving non-charismatic species are routinely
unannounced, even in conservation media.

**6. Example species.** Dodo, thylacine (Tasmanian tiger),
passenger pigeon, Steller's sea cow, great auk, Yangtze river
dolphin (baiji), western black rhinoceros, golden toad, Christmas
Island pipistrelle. Imminent: vaquita, Yangtze sturgeon (some
populations), various amphibians.

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Islands disproportionately; historical
North American forests and prairies; freshwater systems; tropical
montane.

**8. Emotional framing.** Grief; irrevocability; guilt;
melancholy; rage.

**9. Narrative framing.** Gone forever; what we lost; the last
one (Lonesome George, Martha the passenger pigeon, Toughie); the
warning.

**10. Research implications.** "Extinction-symbol cycling" — the
process by which new species enter and old species fade from the
extinction-symbol canon — is itself a research subject. Whose
extinctions become memorable, and whose are erased, is a
distributional question with clear methodological treatments.

## 8. Documentary-icon species

**1. Definition.** Species whose contemporary cinematic identity
has been substantially shaped, and often determined, by specific
landmark documentaries. The species "belongs to" the documentary in
public memory.

**2. Cultural visibility.** A single landmark series can embed a
species in cinematic memory for a generation. Planet Earth (BBC
2006), Blue Planet (BBC 2001) and Blue Planet II (2017), The Life
of Birds (1998), March of the Penguins (2005), Our Planet (2019),
and Frozen Planet (2011) function as substantial canon-formers.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Behaviour clips travel beyond
original documentaries, often without attribution. The same
sequences circulate across platforms ("snow leopard chasing markhor
from Planet Earth"), creating a homogenised cinematic identity.
Subsequent productions inherit and reuse framing decisions made by
the originals.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Stylistic homogenisation
across nature media; canonical sequences become obligatory tropes;
species' "documentary face" diverges from ecological reality. The
narrator-mediated mode (Attenborough, Sigourney Weaver) can
displace direct observation.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Species not yet captured by
landmark productions remain pre-iconic regardless of ecological
significance. Slow or cryptic species rarely make it into
documentary canon.

**6. Example species.** Snow leopard (Planet Earth), emperor
penguin (March of the Penguins, Frozen Planet), birds of paradise
(Attenborough/Laman), peregrine falcon (Earthflight), sailfish
(Blue Planet II), naked mole-rat (various), pistol shrimp (Blue
Planet II), Bornean orangutan (numerous productions).

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Alpine, polar, rainforest canopy,
pelagic ocean, deep reef, savanna, abyssal.

**8. Emotional framing.** Awe; reverence; narrator-mediated
wonder; "first time on camera" affect.

**9. Narrative framing.** The expedition; the elusive subject; the
unprecedented sequence; the moment caught.

**10. Research implications.** Documentary-to-internet propagation
patterns are tractable: which sequences travel furthest, which are
re-cut, which are reused without attribution, which generate
derivative content (memes, edits, reaction videos). A well-bounded
corpus problem.

## 9. Internet-viral species

**1. Definition.** Species that achieved significant cultural
circulation through algorithmic social-media virality, frequently
outside or alongside institutional conservation channels.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Virality follows social-media-friendly
appearance (small, expressive, visually unusual), short-form-
friendly behaviour, anthropomorphisable face/posture, and
audio-overdub potential. Institutional conservation visibility is
neither necessary nor sufficient for virality.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube
Shorts, Twitter/X. Out-of-context behaviour clips with overlaid
audio. Anthropomorphic captioning. Pet-context footage outpacing
wild-context footage. The species' wild ecology is often unknown
to viral audiences.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Pet-trade pressure
(well-documented for axolotl, slow loris, pangolin, capybara,
fennec fox, ball python). Behaviour misinterpretation
(slow lorises raising their arms is a defensive posture, frequently
captioned as cute waving). Conservation-status confusion: viral
species are often assumed by audiences to be doing well *because*
they are visible.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Virality is regionally and
demographically biased; non-Anglophone digital cultures produce
parallel viral canons that mainstream Western media coverage
ignores.

**6. Example species.** Capybara, axolotl, slow loris, pangolin
(post-COVID surge), quokka, sand cat, Pallas's cat, fennec fox,
mantis shrimp, sea bunny (Jorunna parva), tardigrade, octopus
(general), specific named individuals (e.g., Pesto the penguin,
Moo Deng the pygmy hippo).

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Variable; often divorced from native
ecosystems in viral form. Frequently zoo-mediated.

**8. Emotional framing.** Delight; amusement; fascination with
strangeness; parasocial attachment.

**9. Narrative framing.** "Look at this little guy"; chaotic-good
animal; weirdcore; unlikely-friendship; "the funniest animal you've
never heard of."

**10. Research implications.** Viral-cycle longitudinal analysis
is well-suited to platform-data studies. Correlating viral peaks
with pet-trade interest (Google Trends, online marketplace data,
seizure records) is a productive cross-source research design.
Viral attention is also a candidate proxy for digital culturomics
work where Google-Books-style corpora are insufficient.

## 10. Game-mediated species

**1. Definition.** Species whose primary public encounter, for many
audiences, is mediated through video games, interactive fiction, or
play-based media rather than through documentary or direct
observation.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Substantial and growing. Cross-
generational familiarity with species can be game-driven (Animal
Crossing: New Horizons added an extensive fish/insect/sea-creature
museum metagame; Pokémon designs draw on real species and feed
back into species recognition; Subnautica and Abzu present
ecologically inspired alien biomes).

**3. Digital-media shaping.** In-game representation, fan art,
modding, speedrun communities, encyclopedic game wikis. Species are
encountered as gameplay elements (collectible, hostile, resource,
companion). Ecological behaviour is subordinated to game design;
the species' identity is partially mechanical.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Mechanical/exaggerated
behaviour overwrites ecological behaviour in player memory. Real
species are conflated with their game counterparts (and with
fictional creatures derived from them). "Catch them all" framing
imports collection logic into species perception.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Species that don't fit common
game-design slots (predator, prey, resource, companion, threat,
collectible) are absent. Plant, fungal, and microbial life is
under-modelled even in eco-themed games.

**6. Example species.** Various deer and wolf species (open-world
RPGs); coelacanth, oarfish, and other "rare catches" (Animal
Crossing); leafy seadragon, anglerfish (Subnautica family);
Pokémon-inspirations (axolotl ↔ Mudkip family, dunsparce ↔ tsuchinoko,
many others); horse breeds (Red Dead Redemption); domestic species
in farming games (Stardew Valley, Harvest Moon).

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Game-world archetypes (forest, ocean,
savanna, desert, snow biome, ruins) that are abstractions of real
biomes.

**8. Emotional framing.** Collection; familiarity; mastery;
companionship; encounter.

**9. Narrative framing.** Encounter; capture; completion; bestiary.

**10. Research implications.** Cross-platform identification
studies — does Animal Crossing fish-encyclopedia engagement transfer
into real-world identification skill or interest? — are tractable
and unstudied. Serious-games-for-conservation research has a
substantial literature; the relationship between *recreational*
game exposure and conservation knowledge is comparatively under-
researched and methodologically promising.

## 11. Culturally mythologised species

**1. Definition.** Species occupying long-standing roles in
mythology, religion, folklore, ritual, or other pre-digital
cultural-symbolic systems, whose digital representation inherits
and modifies those roles.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Pre-digital cultural depth provides a
substrate that digital media draws on but does not author. Visibility
varies by audience: a tiger is mythologised in Hindu, Tungusic, Han
Chinese, Korean, and South Asian Muslim traditions, with substantial
variation; digital media reaches different sub-audiences with
different inherited frames.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Reinforces, recovers, hybridises, or
overwrites local mythologies. Dominant-culture digital media
sometimes flattens regional traditions into a single "world
mythology" register; conversely, digital platforms can amplify
local cultural framings to global audiences (e.g., orang-hutan as
"person of the forest" in Malay reaching Anglophone audiences).

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Dominant-culture mythologies
overwrite local ones. The Western/Anglophone reading of a species
becomes default. Romanticised "ancient wisdom" framings can
flatten the actual cultural complexity.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Many regional mythologies do
not propagate to global digital audiences and are effectively
inaccessible outside their language community. Oral traditions
that have not been digitised receive systematically lower
representation.

**6. Example species.** Tiger (Hindu, Korean, Chinese, Tungusic
traditions); wolf (European folklore, Indigenous American
traditions, Mongolian mythology); eagle (Mexica, Roman, U.S., many
others); salmon (Pacific Northwest Indigenous, Celtic, Japanese);
serpent (near-globally significant); jaguar (Mesoamerican); raven
(Northwest Coast Indigenous, Norse, Welsh); whale (Inuit, Maori,
Pacific Northwest); cattle (Hindu, Maasai, Egyptian).

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Cultural geography rather than ecology.
Categories of habitat (mountain, forest, river, ocean) carry their
own symbolic weight independent of specific species.

**8. Emotional framing.** Reverence; sacredness; fear-respect;
ancestral connection; ritual obligation.

**9. Narrative framing.** Trickster; guardian; ancestor; omen;
deity-vehicle; teacher.

**10. Research implications.** Comparative cultural-narrative
analysis across language corpora is a defined area of digital
humanities. Digital culturomics work has begun on multilingual
Google Books and Wikipedia corpora; species-and-myth corpus studies
remain comparatively open.

## 12. Conflict species

**1. Definition.** Species in chronic, ecologically structural
conflict with human livelihoods or safety: livestock predation,
crop damage, property damage, vector-borne disease, fisheries
competition, dangerous encounter.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Conflict events generate news cycles.
Photogenic incidents (elephants in villages, leopards in cities,
sharks at beaches) are over-covered relative to chronic conflict
(slow agricultural damage, livestock loss in remote areas).

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Viral footage of conflict events.
Smartphone-recorded incidents propagate rapidly. Local mob-justice
footage can circulate globally. News framing alternates between
"wildlife threat to humans" and "humans threatening wildlife"
modes; the same species can be both hero and villain across
platforms in the same week.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Wildlife framed as criminal
agents. Root causes (habitat compression, resource competition,
infrastructural placement) suppressed. Photogenic conflict events
displace structural conflict from public attention.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Human victims of conflict —
particularly in low-income regions where wildlife conservation has
external advocacy — are systematically under-platformed in global
conservation media. Their experience appears mainly in local
journalism, rarely propagating to international audiences.

**6. Example species.** Asian elephant (crop raiding); leopard
(urban encounters in India); spotted hyena (livestock); great
white shark (beach encounters); sea lion (fisheries); wild boar
(crops, urban); American black bear (waste, property); mountain
lion (urban edge); saltwater crocodile (riverine); chimpanzee
(crop, in some regions); coyote (urban); macaque (peri-urban).

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Edge habitats; agricultural-wildland
interface; peri-urban; riparian-and-village; coastal-and-fishery.

**8. Emotional framing.** Fear; frustration; resentment;
intermittent sympathy; political resentment.

**9. Narrative framing.** Villain; survivor; displaced victim;
the encroacher (in either direction); the misunderstood.

**10. Research implications.** Conflict-coverage bias studies —
*whose* loss is shown, in *whose* language, with *whose* framing —
are tractable and politically consequential. Tracking shifts in
framing language over time and across regions is a productive
area for environmental-communication research.

## 13. Neglected biodiversity

**1. Definition.** Taxa for which ecological evidence indicates
substantial functional importance but whose representation in
mainstream digital media is systematically minimal. The category
is defined negatively: it is what the rest of the taxonomy has
been ignoring.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Low. Often confined to specialist
niches: mycology channels, entomology Twitter, soil-ecology
podcasts, professional taxonomy networks. Low visibility is the
defining characteristic.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Specialist representation only.
Mainstream nature documentary occasionally features charismatic
exceptions (leafcutter ants, mantis shrimp) but does not represent
the underlying biodiversity at scale. Algorithmic systems do not
amplify what they have not been trained on; the absence
self-reinforces.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** N/A by definition.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** This is the most consequential
representational risk in conservation communication. Public
ecological literacy has no model for these taxa; conservation
funding follows visibility; their decline can proceed entirely
without cultural registration; biodiversity policy treats
non-charismatic taxa as accounting placeholders rather than living
subjects.

**6. Example species/groups.** Most insects (an estimated >1
million described species, vs. ~6,400 mammals); nematodes (the
most numerous animals on Earth by individuals); soil microbiota;
fungi (estimated 2-4 million species, ~150,000 described);
lichens; the bulk of plant diversity beyond a few iconic taxa;
marine and freshwater invertebrates broadly; deep-sea fauna;
microbial mat communities; Archaea and most bacteria.

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Soil; leaf litter; deep ocean;
freshwater interstitial; cave; microbial mat; root rhizosphere;
canopy invertebrate fauna.

**8. Emotional framing.** Typically none — neglect itself is the
framing. When present: wonder at scale; defamiliarisation; "hidden
world" affect.

**9. Narrative framing.** "The hidden world"; "the forgotten
kingdom"; "what's beneath your feet"; the catalogue of strangeness.

**10. Research implications.** The most important category for
gap-analysis frameworks. A platform purporting to be conservation-
communication-aware must be able to *measure* and *report* its own
representation of these taxa. Comparing platform coverage to
benchmark taxa (described species in GBIF, IUCN-assessed taxa,
biomass distribution) yields a quantitative bias index.

## 14. "Ugly" or unpopular species

**1. Definition.** Species whose appearance, behaviour, or
ecological niche elicits aversive aesthetic responses (disgust,
revulsion, dismissal) from mainstream human audiences. The category
overlaps with neglected biodiversity but is distinguished by
*active* aesthetic rejection rather than mere absence.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Low, with episodic counter-campaign
visibility (Ugly Animal Preservation Society; "Save the [X]"
campaigns explicitly framed against aesthetic bias). Aesthetic
gating operates at multiple stages: producer selection, platform
algorithmic ranking, audience attention.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** When represented, often through a
"misunderstood" or "fascinating once you look closely" reframing.
Defamiliarisation strategies (extreme close-up, slowed-down
behaviour) can redeem aesthetic perception. Without deliberate
framing, default perception persists.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Almost never overrepresented.
The "ugly chic" niche exists but is small.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Reduced conservation funding
correlated with aesthetic preference is well-documented (Smith et
al. 2012; Adamo et al. 2021 and adjacent literature). Biodiversity
policy follows aesthetic in measurable ways. Some of the most
ecologically important species — vultures, decomposer fungi,
parasitoid wasps — sit in this category.

**6. Example species.** Blobfish (the standard ironic exemplar);
aye-aye; vultures (multiple species); naked mole-rat (despite
documentary visibility); hagfish; lampreys; leeches; many
parasitoid wasps; bot flies; angler fish (males particularly);
giant isopods; certain cave-dwelling salamanders; many
amphibians beyond the photogenic frogs.

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Deep sea; caves; scavenger-decomposer
niches; parasitic relationships; subterranean.

**8. Emotional framing.** Disgust; curiosity; defensive sympathy;
"misunderstood underdog" affect when reframed.

**9. Narrative framing.** Underdog; misunderstood; "fascinating
once you look closely"; the unsung; ecological-job-no-one-wants.

**10. Research implications.** The "aesthetic premium" in
conservation funding is empirically tractable: regression of
funding against visibility-controlled aesthetic rating is a
clean dataset. Whether digital-media intervention can shift the
premium is an open question with implications for conservation
strategy.

## 15. Ecosystem-indicator species

**1. Definition.** Species whose population health is used as an
empirical signal for broader ecosystem condition (sensu indicator,
sentinel, and umbrella-species concepts; the three are
distinguishable but frequently conflated in popular media).

**2. Cultural visibility.** Scientific rather than aesthetic
visibility. The "canary in the coal mine" frame is widespread.
Some indicator species are charismatic enough to anchor coverage
(salmon, bees, polar bears for sea ice); many are not (lichens,
midges, specific frog species).

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Science-journalism contexts more
than entertainment. Coverage tends to follow population-decline
moments rather than chronic indication; indicator status often
flattens into "they're disappearing" framing without ecosystem
context.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Indicator role
mis-communicated as direct causation ("bees are dying so
agriculture will collapse"). The complexity of indicator
relationships (which species indicates *what* about *which*
ecosystem variable) is flattened. Multiple indicators are conflated
under "the planet is sick."

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Less-charismatic indicators
(lichens for air quality; specific aquatic invertebrates for
freshwater quality; fungal indicators; soil-invertebrate
indicators) are systematically under-covered.

**6. Example species.** Honey bee and native solitary bees
(pollination); amphibians broadly (water and land quality);
salmon (river and ocean quality); coral (reef and ocean
acidification); bats (cave and insect-population health); lichens
(air quality); freshwater mussels; predatory birds (DDT-era
canonical, ongoing for some pollutants); migratory shorebirds.

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Wetland; riparian; coral reef;
agricultural; cave; forest; coastal estuary.

**8. Emotional framing.** Concern; scientific authority; warning.

**9. Narrative framing.** The canary; the sentinel; the warning
sign; "if X is dying, we're next."

**10. Research implications.** Whether indicator framing in
digital media remains decoupled from charisma — i.e., whether
non-charismatic indicators ever become the primary framing
species — is an open question. Tracking the proportion of
indicator-framed coverage attached to charismatic vs.
non-charismatic indicators yields a measurable signal of media
ecological depth.

## 16. Meme / mascot species

**1. Definition.** Species whose digital-cultural form is
substantially a meme, brand mascot, sports mascot, or
institutional identity-marker, often with limited or distorted
connection to ecological reality.

**2. Cultural visibility.** High in mascot form; the mascot may
be more recognisable than the species. Brand-and-meme visibility
is governed by graphic-design and branding logics, not by
ecological criteria.

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Memetic propagation; brand
licensing; sports-team identity; plush merchandise; emoji and
sticker presence. The mascot identity is durable across decades
(WWF panda, since 1961) and operates somewhat independently of
the species' ecological situation.

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** The species' ecology becomes
secondary to its brand. Public knowledge consists of mascot
attributes, not behavioural or ecological attributes. Political
mascotting (a species adopted as a political identity-marker) can
weaponise the species' representation.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** When a species becomes a
mascot, other species in the same genus or family can become
relatively invisible (the WWF panda is more visible than other
ursids; eagles-as-national-symbol over other raptors).

**6. Example species.** Giant panda (WWF); various bald, golden,
and harpy eagles (national symbols); falcons, gators, tigers
(sports mascots); peregrine falcon (logo of multiple
institutions); grumpy cat, lil bub (individual meme animals);
specific named individuals turned mascots (Knut, Sudan, Cecil);
penguin (Linux); Hello Kitty proximity (cats broadly); Pepe-
adjacent symbols (frogs, with significant complications).

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Often divorced from ecology; the
mascot exists in branding-space.

**8. Emotional framing.** Affection; in-group identity; nostalgia;
brand loyalty.

**9. Narrative framing.** Identity-marker; brand asset; in-joke;
heritage symbol.

**10. Research implications.** Brand-versus-ecology dissonance is
measurable: surveys of mascot-recognition vs. ecological-
knowledge among the same audiences yield a clean measure of
representational substitution. Useful for digital humanities work
on brand-mediated naturalism.

## 17. Conservation-campaign species

**1. Definition.** Species explicitly selected by conservation
NGOs, governmental bodies, or coalitions as the focus of named,
funded, time-bounded campaigns. The category overlaps heavily with
flagship megafauna but is distinguished by the *campaign* as the
unit of analysis.

**2. Cultural visibility.** Institutionally amplified.
Co-ordinated communications strategies, branded hashtags, donation
portals, dedicated content. Campaigns frequently produce their own
documentary vehicles (e.g., the TX2 campaign and tiger
documentaries; vaquita campaigns and *Sea of Shadows*).

**3. Digital-media shaping.** Campaign content is
SEO-and-platform-optimised; species pages, donation flows, social
templates. Influencer partnerships. Branded content and editorial
content blur. Hashtag-driven attention bursts on key dates
(World Tiger Day, World Pangolin Day, etc.).

**4. Risks of overrepresentation.** Campaign success measured in
fundraising rather than ecosystem outcomes. "Single-species
solutionism" — the campaign frames the ecosystem's situation as a
species' situation. Donor fatigue and campaign-burnout cycles.
Multiple campaigns competing for the same donor attention
flatten into generic urgency.

**5. Risks of underrepresentation.** Species without institutional
campaign sponsorship are relatively invisible regardless of
ecological need. Smaller and regional NGOs reach narrower
audiences than international organisations; the campaign
landscape itself has a charisma bias.

**6. Example species.** Giant panda (WWF founding campaign,
ongoing); tiger (TX2 doubling campaign, 2010-2022; subsequent
TX2.5); African elephant (multiple anti-ivory campaigns);
pangolin (anti-trafficking campaigns post-2010); monarch
butterfly (Pollinator Highway, Monarch Joint Venture); vaquita
(Sea Shepherd, multiple); orangutan (Borneo Orangutan Survival
Foundation; deforestation campaigns); rhinoceros (multiple
species, multiple campaigns); North Atlantic right whale
(ongoing fishery-entanglement campaigns).

**7. Typical ecosystems.** Those associated with named campaigns;
disproportionately tropical forest, savanna, marine, and Arctic.

**8. Emotional framing.** Urgency; hope-tinged-with-loss;
empowerment-through-donation; scarcity.

**9. Narrative framing.** Rescue; doubling; saving the last [N];
the deadline; the turnaround.

**10. Research implications.** Campaign-effectiveness vs.
visibility decoupling is a substantial open research area.
Fundraising metrics and ecological outcomes are often available
but rarely jointly analysed. Longitudinal tracking of campaign-
species visibility through hashtag corpora, news archives, and
search-term data yields publishable datasets in conservation
communication and digital culturomics.

## Cross-cutting notes for use of this taxonomy

### Multi-tagging worked example

A single species typically occupies multiple categories
simultaneously. The tiger is:

- flagship megafauna (1)
- charismatic predator (2)
- documentary-icon (8)
- culturally mythologised (11)
- conflict species (12)
- conservation-campaign species (17)
- meme/mascot species (16) in some contexts

Of the 17 categories, the tiger reliably tags 7. By contrast, a
soil nematode tags only 13 (neglected biodiversity), and possibly
nothing else. The tag-count distribution across species is itself
a research variable; species with high tag-counts are
overrepresented in the platform's *categorical attention*, which
is a distinct measure from raw coverage volume.

### What the taxonomy does not classify

- **Sub-specific entities** (subspecies, populations, individuals).
  A named individual (Cecil the lion, Sudan the rhino) carries
  meme/mascot energy that the species level does not. A successor
  framework should add an entity-tier above the species level
  (population/individual/species/genus/higher-taxon).
- **Habitat-level representation.** The taxonomy is species-centred.
  A complementary habitat-coverage typology should be authored
  separately and cross-referenced.
- **Producer entities.** Who produces the representation (BBC, a
  TikTok creator, a national park, a regional NGO, a researcher
  with a smartphone) is an independent dimension and not classified
  here.
- **Audience entities.** The audience for whom representation
  circulates is an independent dimension; visibility in one
  language, region, or platform does not transfer.

### Suggested companion artefacts (for future authoring)

- **Digital-content-ecosystem typology** — classifies media forms
  rather than species (documentary, short-form, livestream, game,
  etc.).
- **Narrative-technique taxonomy** — classifies storytelling moves
  rather than species (anthropomorphism, catastrophe framing,
  scientific-authority narration, etc.).
- **Habitat-coverage typology** — biome-and-ecosystem level
  representation.
- **Bias-measurement framework** — quantitative methods for
  measuring representational gaps across the taxonomies.

These are out of scope for this document but are the natural
companions for a complete species-media-narrative research matrix.

### Maintenance discipline

This taxonomy is a working draft. Amendments should:

- Preserve the ten-field structure for each category.
- Date and justify any added, merged, or retired category.
- Cite at least one source — peer-reviewed where possible — for any
  claim that goes beyond well-established conservation-communication
  consensus.
- Resist the temptation to expand category count without need.
  Categorical inflation reduces analytical traction.

### Closing note on use

The category names in this taxonomy are intentionally legible to
conservation-communication and environmental-media-studies
audiences. Adoption requires no new vocabulary; the categories
should be useful in literature reviews, methods sections, and
coding schemes for content analysis with minimal adaptation.

The taxonomy's success criterion is not how cleanly it classifies
familiar species; it is whether it makes systematic gaps visible
for species and ecosystems that the broader conservation-media
landscape has not classified at all.
