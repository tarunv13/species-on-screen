# Emerging Research Hypotheses

> Provisional analytical claims surfaced by the research-matrix
> track to date (PRs #19–#23). Stated as testable hypotheses with
> explicit falsification criteria.
>
> **Status.** Working draft. Hypotheses are emerging, not
> established. The methodology that would test them
> (`pilot-corpus-plan.md`, PR #22) has not yet been executed.
>
> **Not.** Not findings. Not theory. Not policy claims. The
> document's purpose is to make the strongest hypotheses
> articulable and falsifiable so subsequent empirical work has
> something concrete to test against.

---

## Preamble

### What "hypothesis" means here

Each entry below is a claim that:

1. Is *testable* in principle, with study designs at least
   sketched.
2. Is *falsifiable* — explicit conditions under which the
   hypothesis would be rejected are stated.
3. Emerges from the research-matrix work, not from prior
   committed positions.
4. Is *provisional* — the research-matrix track has not yet
   produced the empirical evidence needed to support or refute
   any of these claims at scale.

This is the central methodological caution: the hypotheses are
analytically motivated and partially supported by adjacent
literatures, but the corpus evidence the research-matrix track
was designed to produce has not yet been collected. Treat every
"the available evidence is consistent with…" formulation
accordingly.

### Register

The document avoids certainty language deliberately. Phrases
like "may," "appears to," "is consistent with but does not
establish," "would be falsified if," and "tentatively suggests"
are not hedges; they are accurate descriptions of the evidential
state.

### Field schema for each hypothesis

1. **Core claim** — the testable proposition.
2. **Why it matters** — why settling the claim would change
   conservation-communication practice or research design.
3. **Current evidence** — what in PRs #19–#23 and adjacent
   literatures is consistent with the claim. Limits explicit.
4. **Falsifying evidence** — what observation would reject the
   hypothesis.
5. **Most relevant media forms** — where the hypothesis is most
   testable.
6. **Comparative studies** — concrete designs that could test
   it.

## H1 — Spectacle and ecological understanding are empirically independent dimensions

### 1. Core claim

Across digital biodiversity media artefacts, spectacle intensity
and structural/ecological-understanding content are independent
dimensions, not endpoints of a single continuum. An artefact's
score on one is not predictive of its score on the other.

### 2. Why it matters

If true, the standard practice of evaluating conservation media
on a single quality axis ("good" vs "bad" / "engaging" vs
"didactic") collapses two dimensions that should be measured
separately. Evaluation, funding, and editorial decisions
predicated on the single-axis assumption misallocate effort.
Independence also reframes the spectacle-vs-substance debate:
the question is not which to choose but how each is calibrated.

### 3. Current evidence

The three-packet stress test in PR #23 produced (S, U) scores of
(5, 2), (3, 1), and (1, 1) — none of which fall on a single
spectacle-substance axis. The audience-effects framework (PR
#20) classifies twelve media forms across the two dimensions
independently and produces twelve distinct combinations rather
than a one-dimensional ordering. Adjacent literatures support
the conceptual independence: blue-chip documentary research
(Bousé; Mills) describes high-spectacle low-structural-content
patterns; citizen-science research (Bonney et al.; Dickinson
et al.) describes the opposite. The evidence is *consistent with*
independence; it does not yet *establish* it at corpus scale.

### 4. Falsifying evidence

A correlation of |r| > 0.5 between spectacle and
ecological-understanding scores across a coded corpus of
N ≥ 200 artefacts would weaken the independence claim
substantially. A correlation of |r| > 0.7 would falsify it.
Within a single form (e.g., nature documentary alone), strong
within-form correlation would not falsify the cross-form
independence claim but would suggest the dimensions covary
within particular production traditions.

### 5. Most relevant media forms

All twelve forms in PR #20 are relevant; the test requires
sampling across forms so that the full (S, U) plane is
populated. Within-form testing alone risks missing the
cross-form structure that underwrites independence.

### 6. Comparative studies

- **Cross-form scatter analysis** of (S, U) scores from a
  corpus of N ≥ 200 across the twelve forms; principal-
  component analysis of the resulting two-dimensional
  distribution.
- **Inter-coder reliability** on both scales, computed
  independently per scale, to confirm that any observed
  correlation is not an artefact of coder behaviour.
- **Within-genre subspace analysis** for the two genres
  most likely to violate independence (blue-chip documentary;
  citizen-science platforms), comparing within-genre
  covariance to cross-genre.

## H2 — Emotional engagement and behavioural change are decoupled in the absence of specific structural conditions

### 2.1. Core claim

High emotional engagement with biodiversity content does not
reliably produce durable behavioural change unless the engagement
is accompanied by at least one of: active cognitive participation,
structural ecological content as the medium of delivery, or
sustained / repeated exposure. In the absence of all three, even
intense emotional engagement decays without behavioural
consequence.

### 2.2. Why it matters

If true, a substantial portion of conservation-communication
practice — the production budget for emotionally optimised
documentary, the campaign emphasis on awe-and-grief affect, the
investment in viral emotional moments — is misallocated relative
to the behavioural outcomes it claims. Re-allocation toward
participation, structural delivery, or sustained engagement
would follow.

### 2.3. Current evidence

The KAB-gap and value-action-gap literatures (Kollmuss & Agyeman
2002; Steg & Vlek; subsequent meta-analyses) support a general
decoupling between affective engagement and durable behaviour
change in environmental contexts. The audience-effects framework
(PR #20) identifies the three structural conditions as common
properties of forms with documented behaviour-change evidence
(citizen science, serious games, environmental journalism). Forms
high in emotional engagement but lacking the structural
conditions (TikTok virality, Instagram aesthetics, blue-chip
documentary) show short-term spike effects (search behaviour,
donation timing) but limited durable change in the existing
literature. The evidence is consistent but not corpus-validated.

### 2.4. Falsifying evidence

A longitudinal study (≥ 12 months) of audiences exposed to
high-emotional / low-structural / single-exposure biodiversity
content showing durable behavioural change at a magnitude
comparable to participatory or structurally-rich forms would
falsify the claim. A robust replication of the "Blue Planet
effect" beyond search-volume and self-report — i.e., showing
durable consumer-behaviour change attributable to single-
exposure documentary viewing — would substantially weaken it.

### 2.5. Most relevant media forms

Nature documentaries; TikTok virality; Instagram aesthetics;
conservation campaigns. Forms that are high-engagement and
low-participation are the discriminating cases.

### 2.6. Comparative studies

- **Matched-cohort longitudinal study** of equivalent audiences
  exposed to (a) emotionally optimised documentary,
  (b) emotionally optimised serious game, (c) participatory
  citizen-science onboarding, with behaviour-tracking at 1, 6,
  and 12 months.
- **Quasi-experimental analysis** of natural exposure cohorts
  around major broadcast events (a Planet Earth episode; a
  campaign launch) using pre/post-period donation, search,
  and (where available) consumer-behaviour panel data.
- **Replication of "Blue Planet effect"-style claims** with
  corpus-derived comparison artefacts to test whether the
  claimed effect is form-specific or content-specific.

## H3 — Documentary authority is being eroded by streaming-context fragmentation

### 3.1. Core claim

The same documentary content, encountered in algorithmic-
catalogue streaming context, produces weaker conservation-
relevant outcomes than the same content encountered in
broadcast or curated-festival context. The erosion is not in
the artefact but in the consumption ecology around it.

### 3.2. Why it matters

If true, conservation-documentary commissioning strategies that
assume streaming reach equates to impact are misallocated. The
production-side decision to underwrite streaming documentaries
is rational on visibility metrics; if outcome-per-viewer is
substantially lower than for broadcast, total impact may be
flat or declining despite increased reach.

### 3.3. Current evidence

The audience-effects framework (PR #20) treats streaming and
broadcast as distinct forms with distinct emotional and
attentional characteristics. The conceptual case is strong:
streaming-mode viewing is fragmented (interleaved with
non-documentary content), recommender-driven (audiences arrive
without editorial framing), and recall-poor (audiences cannot
attribute viewing to platform or producer). Empirical evidence
is sparse — most documentary-effects studies have not
distinguished broadcast from streaming context. The hypothesis
is a structural prediction, not yet an empirical observation.

### 3.4. Falsifying evidence

Matched-content audience studies showing equivalent or stronger
behavioural and comprehension outcomes from streaming-context
exposure compared to broadcast-context exposure of the same
documentary would falsify the claim. If the difference were
small (effect-size d < 0.2) and confidence intervals included
zero, the hypothesis would be substantially weakened.

### 3.5. Most relevant media forms

Nature documentaries that have been distributed in both
broadcast and streaming forms (Planet Earth franchises;
Frozen Planet; *Our Planet* on Netflix vs hypothetical
broadcast equivalents). The hypothesis cannot be tested on
streaming-only documentaries (My Octopus Teacher) because the
broadcast counterfactual is unavailable.

### 3.6. Comparative studies

- **Cross-context matched audience study**: same documentary,
  randomly assigned to broadcast-style or streaming-style
  consumption context, with comprehension and attitude measures
  at multiple time points.
- **Natural-experiment analysis** of documentaries that
  transitioned from broadcast to streaming distribution,
  comparing audience-effect studies before and after the
  transition where comparable instruments exist.
- **Recommender-context priming studies** isolating the effect
  of cross-genre placement (documentary preceded by thriller
  vs preceded by educational content) on subsequent
  comprehension and attitude outcomes.

## H4 — Algorithmic short-form virality is structurally biased toward conservation-negative outcomes for the species it features

### 4.1. Core claim

Within the short-form algorithmic ecosystem (TikTok, Reels,
Shorts), virality of a wildlife species is more frequently
correlated with negative or null conservation outcomes for that
species (pet-trade pressure, behaviour misinterpretation,
welfare-problematic emulation) than with positive outcomes. The
correlation is not the result of bad actors but of structural
selection pressures: the features that drive virality
(out-of-context cuteness, anthropomorphisable behaviour,
domestic-context proximity) are also the features that
correlate with conservation-negative reception.

### 4.2. Why it matters

If true, conservation-communication strategies that use short-
form virality as an engagement objective are not merely
ineffective but counter-productive. Conservation
communicators investing in TikTok presence may be amplifying
the very dynamics that harm their target species. Conversely,
species absent from short-form virality may benefit from the
absence rather than suffer from invisibility.

### 4.3. Current evidence

The slow-loris literature (Nekaris et al. 2013, 2015, 2020)
provides the strongest documented case: viral short-form video
correlates with measurable pet-trade pressure on Endangered and
Critically Endangered species. The pattern recurs across multiple
species (axolotl; capybara; pangolin; fennec fox), each with
adjacent welfare and trafficking literature. The audience-effects
framework (PR #20) classifies TikTok virality with an explicit
"sometimes-negative" behavioural-effect rating. The exemplar
coding packet (PR #23 #2) operationalises this for a specific
genre. The available evidence is consistent with the structural
claim but is drawn from a small set of well-documented species;
the generalisation has not been corpus-tested.

### 4.4. Falsifying evidence

A corpus-scale analysis showing that viral short-form attention
on a given species reliably correlates with positive
conservation outcomes (donations, policy change, habitat
protection investment) at a magnitude exceeding the negative
effects (pet-trade volume, welfare-problematic emulation) would
falsify the claim. Identification of multiple species for which
short-form virality produced durable conservation-positive
effect *without* corresponding negative effect would
substantially weaken it.

### 4.5. Most relevant media forms

TikTok virality; Instagram Reels; YouTube Shorts. The hypothesis
specifically targets algorithmic short-form; it does not extend
to YouTube long-form ecology channels or to platform-mediated
livestreams.

### 4.6. Comparative studies

- **Correlation analysis** between short-form virality cycles
  (per-species) and time-series data on pet-trade volume,
  online marketplace listings, customs seizure records, and
  welfare-problematic content prevalence.
- **Comparative case-study analysis** across species that have
  experienced viral peaks, distinguishing those whose downstream
  effects are conservation-positive from those whose effects are
  null or negative; identifying the species-level features
  associated with positive vs negative outcomes.
- **Producer-intent analysis** comparing viral content from
  conservation-organisation accounts (intentional conservation
  framing) with viral content from non-conservation accounts
  (incidental wildlife framing), testing whether intentional
  framing alters outcome distribution.

## H5 — Charismatic-species concentration is stable or growing despite institutional efforts to diversify

### 5.1. Core claim

Across digital biodiversity media, the volume of attention
allocated to a small set of charismatic flagship species
(roughly 30–50 vertebrate species) is stable or increasing as a
proportion of total biodiversity-media coverage, despite
explicit institutional efforts (broadcaster commissioning, NGO
campaign portfolios, science-communication outreach) to
diversify representation toward neglected biodiversity.

### 5.2. Why it matters

If true, the institutional diversification efforts are not
yielding measurable representational outcomes; the structural
pressures (algorithmic amplification of existing visibility;
producer-side risk-aversion; audience pre-existing familiarity)
are stronger than the deliberate counter-pressures. Conservation
funding and policy attention follow visibility; persistent
concentration of visibility produces persistent concentration of
material support.

### 5.3. Current evidence

The species coverage typology (PR #19) identifies 17 categories;
mainstream media coverage is concentrated in flagship-megafauna,
charismatic-predator, and conservation-campaign categories,
with neglected biodiversity systematically under-represented.
Conservation-marketing literature (Verissimo et al.; Smith &
Sutton) documents the persistence of flagship selection.
Conservation-culturomics work (Ladle et al.) documents
attention-concentration patterns in cultural-data signals.
Direct longitudinal corpus evidence — coverage *over time* —
is sparse; the hypothesis assumes the pattern is at least
stable. Whether it is *growing* is a stronger claim that the
existing evidence does not establish.

### 5.4. Falsifying evidence

Longitudinal corpus analysis showing that the proportion of
biodiversity media coverage allocated to non-flagship taxa has
grown substantially (e.g., from <10% to >25% over a decade)
would falsify the "stable or growing concentration" claim. A
corpus-scale analysis showing that institutional diversification
campaigns produce measurable shifts in subsequent media coverage
volume (within 1–3 years of campaign launch) would substantially
weaken it.

### 5.5. Most relevant media forms

Nature documentaries; conservation campaigns; environmental
journalism; mainstream Instagram and YouTube. The hypothesis is
about media concentration *across* these forms; testing requires
multi-form longitudinal sampling.

### 5.6. Comparative studies

- **Longitudinal corpus volume analysis**: per-species media
  volume across forms across multi-year windows, normalised
  against species richness and assessed conservation status.
- **Institutional-campaign impact study**: pre/post-period
  coverage analysis around named diversification campaigns
  (Pollinator-focused initiatives; invertebrate-of-the-year
  programmes; fungal-conservation efforts), testing whether
  named campaigns produce measurable downstream coverage shifts.
- **Producer-side decision study**: editorial-choice analysis
  of broadcaster commissioning over time, testing whether stated
  diversification commitments are reflected in commissioned
  coverage.

## H6 — Behaviour change requires participation, not exposure

### 6.1. Core claim

Among the twelve media forms in PR #20, durable conservation-
relevant behavioural change is reliably produced only by forms
that require *active participation* from audiences (citizen
science; serious games with mechanism-bound learning;
sustained-engagement YouTube channels with parasocial
investment). Passive forms — regardless of emotional intensity,
production value, or reach — do not reliably produce durable
behaviour change at meaningful magnitudes.

### 6.2. Why it matters

If true, the distinction between "engagement" (which passive
forms can produce well) and "behaviour change" (which they
cannot) is structural, not contingent. Conservation strategies
that aim to convert passive viewers into actors via emotional
intensity alone are systematically misaligned with the form's
capacity. The implication is not that passive forms have no
value but that their value is bounded at awareness and emotional
engagement, not at behavioural outcome.

### 6.3. Current evidence

The audience-effects framework (PR #20) identifies three
properties shared by forms with documented durable
behaviour-change effects: active cognitive engagement;
structural content as medium; sustained engagement. The
participatory forms (citizen science, serious games) match all
three; passive forms typically match none. Citizen-science
participation literature (Dickinson et al.; Bonney et al.) is
the most robust empirical body, with documented multi-year
behavioural and skill-formation effects. Serious-games
literature is domain-specific but consistent (Sandbrook et al.).
The hypothesis is consistent with a substantial existing
literature but the cross-form comparison has not been done at
corpus scale.

### 6.4. Falsifying evidence

A passive form (single-exposure documentary; algorithmic
short-form virality; campaign exposure) showing durable
behaviour change at a magnitude comparable to participatory
forms when matched on content quality and exposure duration
would falsify the participation-threshold claim. A reliable
counter-example — a single passive-form intervention with
multi-year matched-cohort behaviour evidence — would force
revision.

### 6.5. Most relevant media forms

The hypothesis is cross-form by construction. Specifically
testable along the participatory↔passive continuum: citizen
science vs nature documentary vs TikTok vs serious games.

### 6.6. Comparative studies

- **Matched-effort cross-form study**: equivalent audiences and
  equivalent total exposure time, randomly assigned across
  three forms (e.g., 5 hours of nature documentary; 5 hours of
  serious game; 5 hours of citizen-science onboarding +
  contribution), with behaviour-tracking at 6, 12, 24 months.
- **Effort-controlled meta-analysis**: synthesis of existing
  intervention studies normalised on exposure duration, with
  form as moderator.
- **Form-transition studies**: longitudinal cohorts that
  transition from passive to participatory engagement, testing
  whether form transition is the active ingredient or whether
  pre-existing motivation is.

## H7 — Aesthetic engagement may interfere with ecological understanding

### 7.1. Core claim

Sustained exposure to high-aesthetic / low-structural
biodiversity content may not merely fail to produce ecological
understanding but actively interfere with it, by training the
audience as aesthetic appreciator-of-nature rather than as
participant-in-or-component-of ecosystems. The interference is
hypothesised to operate through habit formation: repeated
positioning of the audience as observer-of-beauty makes
participatory and structural framings feel disjunctive rather
than complementary.

### 7.2. Why it matters

If true, the conservation-communication assumption that
emotional/aesthetic engagement is a *foundation* on which
later ecological understanding can be built is reversed: the
foundation is potentially obstructive. Audience curation
strategies — funnelling audiences from documentary viewing to
participatory action — would need rethinking.

### 7.3. Current evidence

This is the weakest-evidenced hypothesis in the document. The
audience-effects framework (PR #20) flagged it as an explicit
hypothesis worth investigating but acknowledged the empirical
base is thin. Adjacent literatures are suggestive but not
conclusive: studies on Instagram tourism aesthetics and
real-world behavioural effects on photographed sites; the
"Disneyfication" critique in nature-documentary studies (Bousé;
Mills); research on aesthetic-vs-cognitive framing of
environmental issues. None of these directly tests the
interference claim. The hypothesis is currently a *theoretical
possibility*, not a near-empirical claim.

### 7.4. Falsifying evidence

Experimental designs showing that prior aesthetic-mode exposure
to a given ecological subject produces equivalent or stronger
ecological-understanding gains from subsequent participatory or
structural exposure, compared to control audiences without prior
aesthetic exposure, would falsify the interference claim. A
reliable longitudinal observation that high-aesthetic-exposure
audiences progress to participatory engagement at rates equal to
or exceeding low-aesthetic-exposure audiences would weaken it
substantially.

### 7.5. Most relevant media forms

Instagram aesthetics; high-production-value blue-chip
documentary; tourism-adjacent nature media. The hypothesis is
specifically about *high-aesthetic / low-structural* exposure,
not all aesthetic engagement.

### 7.6. Comparative studies

- **Sequential-exposure experimental study**: random assignment
  of audiences to (a) prior aesthetic exposure + subsequent
  participatory invitation, (b) no prior exposure +
  participatory invitation, with conversion-to-participation as
  the dependent measure.
- **Aesthetic-engagement audience profile study**: longitudinal
  tracking of high-Instagram-engagement audiences over time,
  testing whether they progress to participatory or structural
  engagement at rates predicted by population-baseline.
- **Comprehension-after-aesthetic-exposure study**: test of
  ecological-understanding gain after equivalent exposure
  duration to aesthetic vs structural treatment of the same
  content, comparing both retention and transfer.

## H8 — Notable absences in conservation media are systematic and predictable by form and producer

### 8.1. Core claim

What conservation media omits is not idiosyncratic but follows
predictable patterns: human pastoralism is systematically absent
from BBC-style documentary about Global South subjects;
extractive-industry presence is systematically absent from
blue-chip productions; the cinematic apparatus itself is
systematically absent from the artefacts that depend on it; the
trafficking pipeline is systematically absent from content
featuring captive exotic species. Absences are coherent at the
form-and-producer level and can be coded with measurable
inter-coder reliability.

### 8.2. Why it matters

If true, the systematic absences constitute an analyzable
representational politics independent of what the artefacts
include. Conservation-communication critique can move from
single-artefact reading ("this documentary omitted X") to
structural claim ("this form-producer combination predictably
omits X"). Editorial diversification efforts can be evaluated
against what they enable producers to *include* but also against
which absences they *fail to address*.

### 8.3. Current evidence

The corpus-seed framework (PR #21) makes notable-absences
coding mandatory specifically because absences are analytically
loaded. The exemplar coding packet for Planet Earth II (PR #23
#1) surfaced a coherent set of absences that are predictable
from prior knowledge of BBC commissioning patterns. The wider
critical nature-documentary literature (Mills; Chris; recent
work on Indigenous representation in conservation media)
provides extensive case-study evidence. The claim that absences
are *predictable*, not just present, is corpus-testable but has
not yet been tested at scale.

### 8.4. Falsifying evidence

Coded notable-absences data from a corpus of N ≥ 100 artefacts
showing low inter-coder reliability on absence categories
(Krippendorff's alpha < 0.5 across coders) would suggest
absences are idiosyncratic, not systematic. Showing that
absences vary essentially randomly across the form-and-producer
combinations within the corpus, rather than clustering by
form-and-producer, would falsify the predictability claim.

### 8.5. Most relevant media forms

Forms with sustained editorial control: nature documentaries
(particularly blue-chip); environmental journalism; conservation
campaigns; institutional Instagram. The hypothesis is harder to
test on short-form viral content because absences may be
genuinely idiosyncratic at single-artefact scale.

### 8.6. Comparative studies

- **Inter-coder reliability study** of notable-absences coding
  across a corpus of N ≥ 100, with coders trained against
  anchored exemplars and computed agreement statistics on
  absence categories.
- **Producer-pattern analysis**: clustering of absence patterns
  across forms and producers, testing whether form-producer
  combinations predict absence categories.
- **Counter-example identification**: deliberate search for
  artefacts that include absences typical of their form, to
  establish whether the absences are structural or merely
  conventional.

## H9 — Form properties, not content quality, dominate conservation-communication outcomes

### 9.1. Core claim

When two artefacts of equal content quality (same species, same
ecological argument, comparable production values) are
distributed in different forms, the form's properties (active
cognitive engagement; structural content as medium; sustained
engagement; participation) predict downstream conservation-
relevant outcomes more strongly than the content's quality.
Form is the dominant variable; content quality is a secondary
moderator within form.

### 9.2. Why it matters

If true, the standard conservation-communication assumption
that "good content well-executed" is the lever for impact is
misallocated. Form selection is the more consequential
decision than content optimisation. Producers and funders
choosing between investments in a high-quality documentary, a
high-quality serious game, and a high-quality citizen-science
contribution flow are facing a form-selection question with
different expected outcome distributions per form.

### 9.3. Current evidence

The audience-effects framework (PR #20) summary table shows
substantial cross-form variation in outcome capacity. The form-
behaviour-coupling argument (H6 above) is the strongest
adjacent hypothesis. Cross-form matched-content studies are
rare; the hypothesis is consistent with the existing pattern
but has not been directly tested. The pattern observed in PR
#20 is partly attributable to selection effects: participatory-
form audiences are pre-selected for prior interest, which may
explain a non-trivial portion of the apparent form effect.
Untangling form-effect from selection-effect is one of the
methodologically harder problems in this literature.

### 9.4. Falsifying evidence

Matched-content cross-form studies showing that content quality
explains substantially more variance in outcomes than form does
(form-effect / content-effect ratio < 1) would falsify the form-
dominance claim. Documentary-equivalent outcomes from matched
serious-game or citizen-science exposure would weaken it.

### 9.5. Most relevant media forms

Cross-form by construction. The cleanest test is matched-content
across nature documentary, serious game, and citizen-science
contribution.

### 9.6. Comparative studies

- **Matched-content cross-form experiment**: identical content
  delivered through three forms, with random assignment and
  longitudinal outcome tracking.
- **Form-effect / selection-effect decomposition**: instrumental-
  variable or natural-experiment design (e.g., audiences
  exposed to a given form for non-content reasons, such as
  educational requirement) to isolate form-effect from prior-
  motivation selection.
- **Production-budget controlled comparison**: equivalent
  budgets allocated across forms, comparing outcomes per dollar
  rather than outcomes per audience-member.

## H10 — Negative behavioural outcomes are systematically under-measured in conservation-media research

### 10.1. Core claim

The conservation-communication literature, conservation-media
production practice, and conservation-funding evaluation
systematically under-measure negative behavioural outcomes
(pet-trade pressure; tourism pressure on filmed sites;
welfare-problematic emulation; misinformation propagation)
relative to positive behavioural outcomes (donation;
volunteering; advocacy). The asymmetry is structural: positive
outcomes have institutional sponsors with measurement
incentives; negative outcomes have no equivalent advocacy.

### 10.2. Why it matters

If true, the evidence base from which conservation-communication
practice is informed is biased toward positive outcomes by
omission rather than by genuine effect distribution. Strategies
optimised against this biased evidence are systematically over-
optimistic about their net effect.

### 10.3. Current evidence

The exemplar coding packet for the slow-loris artefact (PR #23
#2) operationalises negative outcomes for one well-documented
case. Adjacent literatures on viral wildlife content (Nekaris
et al.; Macdonald, Burnham et al. on the Cecil-effect ambiguity;
tourism-pressure-on-photographed-sites studies) document
specific negative effects but at small scale. The structural
asymmetry — that positive outcomes are routinely measured and
negative outcomes are routinely not — is a methodological
observation supported by review of conservation-impact reporting
but not yet quantified at corpus scale.

### 10.4. Falsifying evidence

A systematic review of conservation-impact studies showing
roughly equal attention to negative and positive outcomes — and
showing that the absence of documented negative effects in
specific cases reflects genuine absence rather than non-
measurement — would falsify the asymmetry claim.

### 10.5. Most relevant media forms

All forms; the asymmetry is form-independent in principle but
likely most severe for the high-engagement / low-participation
forms (TikTok virality; Instagram aesthetics; campaign content)
where positive-outcome claims are routinely made and negative
outcomes are routinely uncounted.

### 10.6. Comparative studies

- **Systematic review** of conservation-impact studies (1990 –
  present), coding each for whether negative outcomes were
  measured, attempted to be measured, or not addressed.
- **Negative-outcome corpus construction**: a sub-corpus
  specifically of artefacts with documented negative effects,
  with measurement methodology cataloguing.
- **Producer-self-report vs independent-measurement
  comparison**: for artefacts with both producer claims and
  independent evaluation, comparing positive- vs negative-
  outcome detection rates.

## Cross-hypothesis interactions

The hypotheses are not independent. Several interact in ways
that empirical work would need to disentangle:

- **H1 (independence) makes H7 (aesthetic interference) testable.**
  If spectacle and ecological understanding were a single
  dimension, the interference claim would be tautological. The
  independence claim is what gives interference analytical
  traction.
- **H4 (algorithmic inversion) reinforces H5 (charismatic
  concentration).** Algorithmic amplification of viral content
  preferentially selects charismatic taxa; the algorithmic
  pressure compounds with the producer-side flagship pressure.
- **H6 (participation threshold) and H9 (form dominance) cover
  overlapping empirical territory.** H6 is the specific
  participation claim; H9 is the more general form-dominance
  claim. H9 includes H6 as a special case: participation is
  one of the form properties through which form dominates.
- **H2 (emotion-behaviour decoupling) is partially explained
  by H6 (participation threshold).** Decoupling is what one
  observes when the participation threshold is not met. The
  two hypotheses are different framings of an overlapping
  empirical pattern.
- **H8 (absence predictability) and H10 (negative-outcome
  under-measurement) are both about systematic invisibility
  in conservation-communication research.** H8 is about
  representational invisibility; H10 is about evidential
  invisibility. Both predict that the field's evidence base
  is systematically incomplete in predictable ways.

## Methodological caveats applying to all hypotheses

1. **No corpus evidence yet.** The pilot corpus (PR #22) has
   not been executed. Each hypothesis is informed by the three
   exemplar packets in PR #23 plus adjacent literatures; none
   has been corpus-tested.
2. **Selection effects.** Several hypotheses (particularly H6
   and H9) are vulnerable to selection-effect confounds.
   Participatory-form audiences are pre-selected for prior
   interest. Untangling this from form effects requires
   instrumental-variable or natural-experiment designs that
   are scarce in the existing literature.
3. **Outcome heterogeneity.** "Behavioural change" is a
   composite of many possible outcomes (donation; consumer
   behaviour; voting; volunteering; community action). Effects
   may be present in one outcome and absent in another.
   Hypothesis-testing should specify the outcome being tested,
   not aggregate.
4. **Cultural and geographic bounding.** The audience-effects
   framework and the species typology were authored against a
   Global-North-Anglophone evidence base. The hypotheses
   inherit that bounding. Their generalisation across cultures
   and languages is itself a research question.
5. **Asymmetric falsifiability.** Some hypotheses (H1, H8, H9)
   are sharp and falsifiable. Others (H7, H10) are broader and
   harder to falsify cleanly. The asymmetry is genuine, not a
   defect of stating; sharper-falsifiable hypotheses should be
   tested first in a research programme.

## What the hypotheses are not

- **Not findings.** No empirical claim has been corpus-validated
  by the research-matrix track yet.
- **Not theory.** The document does not advance an integrated
  theoretical framework of biodiversity-media effects; it states
  testable claims that any such framework would need to address.
- **Not policy.** Conservation-communication funding decisions
  should not be made on the basis of these hypotheses in their
  current state.
- **Not exhaustive.** Other hypotheses are extractable from PRs
  #19–#23 (e.g., on documentary-canon transfer to derivative
  short-form; on producer-country / subject-country mismatch
  effects; on platform-form methodological discontinuity).
  Future iterations of this document may add or retire
  hypotheses as the evidence base grows.

## Lifecycle of this document

The hypothesis list is provisional and is expected to be
revised after the pilot corpus (PR #22) is executed. Specifically:

- Hypotheses partially supported by pilot evidence remain in
  the list with updated *Current evidence* fields.
- Hypotheses contradicted by pilot evidence are retained but
  marked as falsified, with the contradicting evidence
  documented.
- New hypotheses surfaced by pilot coding are added with the
  same six-field schema.
- Hypotheses that turn out to be untestable at corpus scale
  are flagged as such and retained for theoretical reference.

The document's value is not in being right; it is in making the
research-matrix track's analytical commitments concrete,
falsifiable, and visible enough that the empirical work can
test them rather than ignore them.
